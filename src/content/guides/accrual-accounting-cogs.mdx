If you buy products and resell them on a marketplace, the IRS generally expects you to account for inventory and deduct what you sold through cost of goods sold (COGS), not as an ordinary expense in the year you paid the supplier. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-02"/> <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-08"/> The 2025 rules give smaller sellers a way out: if your average annual gross receipts were $31 million or less for the 3 prior tax years, you can choose not to keep an inventory, though you still need a method that clearly reflects income. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-03"/> <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-05"/> This guide walks through who qualifies, what goes into COGS, and how the numbers move through Schedule C.

## Which publication and which year

Publication 334, *Tax Guide for Small Business*, is labeled for use in preparing 2025 returns, and the 2025 Schedule C instructions repeat the same $31 million test. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-01"/> <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-04"/> Publication 538, the IRS guide to accounting periods and methods, still carries a January 2022 revision and quotes the $26 million figure from that older revision. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-31"/> Both describe the threshold as indexed for inflation. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-03"/> <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-31"/> Use Pub 538 for how the inventory methods work; take the dollar threshold from the publication and instructions for the year you are filing.

## Do you have to keep an inventory?

The baseline rule: when the production, purchase, or sale of merchandise is an income-producing factor in your business, you generally must take inventories into account. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-02"/> For a reseller that is almost always the case. The small business taxpayer exception changes the mechanics, not the requirement to measure income properly.

<Callout type="note" title="Non-incidental does not mean deduct at purchase">
Under the non-incidental materials and supplies treatment, the deduction comes in the year the inventoriable items are first used or consumed in your operations — not automatically when you pay the supplier. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-07"/> For a reseller, stock that is still sitting in a warehouse at year-end has not been consumed.
</Callout>

## What goes into cost of goods sold

For merchandise you buy, cost is the invoice price minus appropriate discounts plus transportation or other charges incurred in acquiring the goods. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-28"/> Two kinds of discount reduce that cost. Trade discounts are the gap between the stated price and the price you actually pay. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-22"/> Cash discounts are amounts a supplier lets you deduct from the invoice for paying promptly. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-32"/>

What counts as "in" your inventory at year-end matters as much as what it cost. Pub 538 includes purchased merchandise once title has passed to you, even if it is still in transit, and goods you have sent out on consignment. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-29"/> For importers, that means a container on the water may already belong in your count, depending on when title transfers under your purchase terms.

| Schedule C Part III line | What it holds |
|---|---|
| 35 — Inventory at beginning of year | Last year's closing inventory; attach an explanation if different |
| 36 — Purchases less personal withdrawals | Merchandise bought for sale, excluding the cost of items you took for personal use |
| 37 — Cost of labor | Labor costs, usually only in manufacturing or mining; never amounts paid to yourself |
| 38 — Materials and supplies | Items such as hardware and chemicals used in manufacturing goods |
| 39 — Other costs | Freight-in on merchandise purchased for sale, integral containers and packages, overhead such as rent, heat, light, power, insurance |
| 40 — Add lines 35–39 | Cost of goods available for sale |
| 41 — Inventory at end of year | Closing inventory, which usually becomes next year's line 35 |
| 42 — Cost of goods sold | Line 40 minus line 41; also entered on line 4 |

Most resellers will have little or nothing on lines 37 and 38; Pub 334 frames those lines around manufacturing. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-11"/> <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-12"/> Line 39 matters more: freight-in on merchandise purchased for sale is listed there. If you bundle, kit or private-label goods, read the container example closely: containers and packages count there when they are an integral part of the product manufactured. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-13"/>

## How COGS flows to the top of Schedule C

Returns and allowances include cash or credit refunds to customers, rebates and other allowances. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-17"/> One detail that catches marketplace sellers: if you collect state or local sales tax that is imposed on you as the seller, the amount collected belongs in gross receipts. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-21"/> See the [sales tax nexus guide](/guides/sales-tax-nexus/) for when marketplaces collect on your behalf.

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical)">
Inputs: beginning inventory $8,000; merchandise purchased $40,000, of which $300 was taken home for personal use; inbound freight $2,500; ending inventory counted at $10,500; gross receipts $95,000; customer refunds $4,000.

- Line 35: $8,000
- Line 36: $40,000 − $300 = $39,700
- Line 39: $2,500
- Line 40: $8,000 + $39,700 + $2,500 = $50,200
- Line 41: $10,500
- Line 42 (and line 4): $50,200 − $10,500 = $39,700
- Line 3: $95,000 − $4,000 = $91,000
- Gross profit: $91,000 − $39,700 = $51,300

The $10,500 still on the shelf is not deducted this year; it becomes next year's line 35.
</Callout>

## Valuing and identifying inventory

Whatever approach you use, the IRS requires that it conform to generally accepted accounting principles for similar businesses and clearly reflect income. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-23"/> Line 33 of Schedule C asks how you valued closing inventory: at cost, at the lower of cost or market, or by another IRS-approved method. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-24"/>

Damaged, returned-unsellable or shelf-worn goods get their own rule. Pub 538 calls goods you cannot sell at normal prices "subnormal" and says to value them at their bona fide selling price minus the direct cost of disposition, whichever method you use. <C id="accrual-accounting-cogs-30"/>

## Common mistakes to check before you file

| Mistake | What the IRS guidance says instead |
|---|---|
| Expensing a full year of stock purchases | COGS is purchases plus beginning inventory minus ending inventory; unsold stock carries forward |
| Leaving out goods in transit | Purchased merchandise counts once title passes, even in transit |
| Counting your own draw as labor | Line 37 excludes any amounts paid to yourself |
| Ignoring personal withdrawals | Exclude the cost of items taken for personal use from purchases |
| Valuing damaged returns at full cost | Subnormal goods: bona fide selling price minus direct cost of disposition |
| Opening inventory that doesn't match last year | Attach an explanation of any difference |

## How to confirm it for your return

Read the inventory and cost of goods sold sections of [IRS Publication 334](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) and the Part III instructions in the [Schedule C instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc) for the tax year you are filing; the gross receipts threshold is indexed, so the figure for a later year may differ. Method detail is in [Publication 538](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p538). This page explains the Schedule C mechanics only; if you are thinking of switching inventory methods, or you file a partnership or corporate return, work it through with a tax professional before filing.
