If you sell in a currency you do not bank in, this is a cost you pay on every disbursement and probably have never measured. It is also the cleanest illustration on this site of why source attribution matters.

## The numbers, and who is publishing them

| Source | Reported ACCS cost | What they sell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Amazon** (own programme page) | Volume-based; 2.0% flat for JPY; worked example at 1.0% | The service |
| Exclusiva | 0.75% to 2.0% by volume and payout currency | — |
| Link My Books | 1.5% | Integration with Payoneer |
| Clickstera | 1.5% to 2.5% | Wise and Payoneer alternatives |
| Titan Network | 2.5% to 4% above mid-market | FX alternatives |
| Seller forum comment | 3% to 4%, plus ~2% bank margin | — |
| World First (presentation) | "Typically 3.75%" | A competing FX service |

**The pattern is not subtle.** The primary source and one independent analysis give 0.75% to 2.0%. Every figure above 2.5% comes from a party selling the alternative.

That does not make the higher figures wrong. Amazon's stated percentage may not capture the full spread, and one source makes a specific and plausible technical criticism: the cost is described as sitting **in the spread rather than in a stated fee**, buried in settlement calculations and difficult to isolate. That is a real mechanism.

But a range of 0.75% to 3.75% across sources is not a measurement problem you can resolve by reading more articles. **You resolve it by measuring your own.**

## How to measure your own, in twenty minutes

This is the only number that matters, and the method is published consistently across sources:

1. **Download three months of settlement reports** showing foreign-currency sales
2. **Compare gross foreign revenue with net settlements in your home currency**, excluding Amazon selling fees
3. **Divide net home currency by gross foreign currency** — that is your effective rate
4. **Compare against historical mid-market rates** on your settlement dates, from a source such as XE or OANDA

One source's benchmark: **if you are consistently trailing mid-market by more than 2%, you are paying too much.**

That threshold is reasonable and it is also the point at which a third-party service becomes worth the setup effort.

## What Amazon's own page actually says

Worth reading directly, because it is the primary source and it contains two things the secondary coverage mostly omits.

**Volume-based pricing.** Amazon's worked example describes a Germany-based seller earning $500,000 on Amazon.com, $300,000 on Amazon.co.uk and $300,000 on Amazon.co.jp. Unlinked, each account is priced on its own volume. **Linked, the total of $1,100,000 is priced together at 1.0%, saving a stated $5,500.**

If you sell across multiple marketplaces and have not linked your global accounts, that is a free reduction available today.

**Rate timing.** The rate applied is the one used by Amazon's third-party provider **on the day the transfer to your bank is initiated** — and for failed disbursements, the day it is re-initiated.

That gives you a small amount of control. Manually initiating a disbursement means choosing the day. Whether timing conversions is worth the attention depends on your volume and your tolerance for watching exchange rates, and for most sellers the honest answer is that it is not — but it is worth knowing the mechanism exists.

## The alternative, and what it actually involves

The recommended structure across sources is the same: **receive in local currency, convert yourself.**

That means opening a receiving account in the marketplace currency through a multi-currency provider, giving Amazon those details instead of your home bank, and converting on your own timing through that provider.

Reported third-party costs cluster **under 0.5%** in some accounts and at **1% to 2%** in others, with one seller-forum comment naming several providers as charging broadly similar rates. As with the ACCS figures, the lowest numbers come from the providers themselves.

**Two secondary benefits that are more concrete than the FX saving:**

**Paying ad spend in local currency.** If you advertise on a foreign marketplace and pay with a home-currency card, your bank converts and charges for it. A local-currency account pays the bill directly. For anyone running meaningful ad spend on a foreign marketplace, this can be worth more than the disbursement saving.

**Holding rather than converting.** If you have costs in that currency — suppliers, VAT payments, agency fees — holding the balance avoids converting twice.

## Two things not to confuse this with

**The buyer-facing currency converter** is a shopper convenience with its own rate. Reporting notes the displayed price is often higher than the buyer's own card would charge, which is a conversion consideration for the customer rather than a cost to you.

**Refunds convert too.** Reporting notes refunds processed at different rates from the original sale can produce unexpected losses. On a volatile pair with a long return window, that is a real if small effect.

## What to do

**Measure your own effective rate.** Twenty minutes with three months of settlement reports. Everything else in this guide is a claim; that is a measurement.

**Link your global accounts** if you sell across marketplaces. Amazon's own example puts the saving at $5,500 on $1.1 million.

**Set the 2% benchmark.** Trailing mid-market by more than that is the trigger to look at alternatives.

**Open a local-currency receiving account** if your foreign volume is meaningful — and use it for ad spend regardless of what you decide about disbursements.

**Do not act on a published percentage**, including the ones in this guide. The range across sources is a factor of five and the parties quoting the high end all sell the remedy.

**This is not financial advice.** Currency exposure, hedging and cross-border payment structuring depend on your volumes and jurisdiction, and a conversation with an accountant familiar with cross-border ecommerce will be worth more than any article.
