Yes, there is a statutory end date. Section 70531 of Public Law 119-21 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) amends section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930, and its subsection (b) amendments take effect on July 1, 2027. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-02" /> CBP describes the result plainly: the Act, enacted July 4, 2025, terminated the de minimis exemption effective July 1, 2027. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-06" />

That date matters less for day-to-day operations than it might seem, because the exemption is already suspended for all shipments by executive order and CBP rule (the current regime is covered in [the 2026 de minimis suspension guide](/guides/de-minimis-suspension-2026/)). What changes in 2027 is the legal footing: a suspension can be modified or revoked by the agency that adopted it, while the statutory amendment does not depend on an executive order staying in place.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page reports what the statute and CBP's notice say. How it affects a specific shipment after the change depends on the entry type used, which your broker or carrier confirms.
</Callout>

## What section 70531 says

The section heading is "Modifications to de minimis entry privilege for commercial shipments," and it amends 19 U.S.C. 1321. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-01" /> It has two parts that work on different timetables.

| Part | What it does | When it takes effect |
|---|---|---|
| Subsection (a) | Adds a new subsection (c) to section 321: civil penalty for misuse of the privilege | 30 days after enactment |
| Subsection (b)(1) | Strikes language in section 321(a)(2) | July 1, 2027 |
| Subsection (b)(2) | Repeals the subsection (c) penalty added by subsection (a) | July 1, 2027 |

*Source: U.S. Government Publishing Office, Public Law 119-21, section 70531, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-03" />

### Subsection (b): the repeal mechanism

Subsection (b)(1) amends section 321(a)(2) by striking the text from "of this Act, or" through "subdivision (2); and" and inserting "of this Act; and". <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-04" /> The statute does this by text surgery rather than a standalone "the exemption is repealed" sentence, so the effect is read from what the struck words did in the old section. CBP's reading, stated in its interim final rule, is that the Act terminated the de minimis exemption effective July 1, 2027. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-06" /> Subsection (b)(3) provides that the subsection (b) amendments take effect on July 1, 2027. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-02" />

The section's own heading limits its subject to "commercial shipments." <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-01" /> Whether a given non-commercial import (for example, a gift) keeps any exemption after July 1, 2027 is a question to answer from the amended statute and CBP's implementing rules at that time, not from this summary.

### Subsection (a): the interim civil penalty

Subsection (a) added a civil penalty for any person who enters, introduces, facilitates, or attempts to introduce an article using the de minimis privilege when the article's importation violates another provision of US customs law. The penalty is up to $5,000 for the first violation and up to $10,000 for each subsequent violation, in addition to any other penalty, and it took effect 30 days after enactment. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-05" />

The penalty is designed to be temporary. Subsection (b)(2) repeals that new subsection (c) as part of the same July 1, 2027 change. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-03" /> Once there is no privilege to misuse, a penalty for misusing it has no work to do.

Executive Order 14388 already provides that de minimis does not apply to covered shipments regardless of value, country of origin, mode of transportation, or method of entry, for goods entered on or after 12:01 a.m. EST on February 24, 2026. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-07" /> CBP's non-postal interim final rule, effective June 24, 2026, writes into 19 CFR 10.151 that the exemption is suspended for all modes other than the postal network until CBP determines otherwise and announces it in the Federal Register. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-08" /> A separate CBP interim final rule for mail shipments took effect July 24, 2026 and references the legislation terminating the exemption effective July 1, 2027. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-09" />

So between now and mid-2027 there are two layers: an executive and regulatory suspension today, and a statute that takes the commercial privilege away on a fixed date. The practical consequence for planning is that a future relaxation of the executive suspension, if one happened, would have a limited shelf life for commercial shipments under the statute as CBP describes it.

Three points follow for anyone reading the two layers side by side. First, the executive order and CBP's rules are what govern an entry filed today; the statute is not yet the operative text. Second, the statutory change is scheduled, not conditional: it does not wait for a CBP finding or a new order. Third, CBP will still need implementing rules for the post-July 2027 regime, which is why the proposed rule below is worth following even if your current entries are running smoothly.

## What CBP is building for the post-de minimis world

CBP's October 8, 2026 proposed rule, "Low-Value Shipments," would change filing requirements for informal entries of goods valued at $2,500 or less, create a new electronic informal entry type for mail, require an additional carrier data element for mail shipments, and add bonding requirements for certain informal entries; comments are due December 7, 2026. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-10" /> For a seller, the useful reading of that proposal is that low-value goods are being moved permanently onto the informal entry track, with a bond behind the filer. The entry choice itself is explained in [Entry Type 11 vs 01](/guides/entry-type-11-vs-01/), and bond options in [customs bonds](/guides/customs-bonds/).

## What it means for marketplace sellers

- **No 2027 cliff for most sellers.** If your goods have already been entering with duty paid since the suspension, July 1, 2027 does not change your cost; it fixes it in statute.
- **Business models built on parcel-level duty-free entry are not coming back on the current text.** Planning around a policy reversal now means planning around a change in law, not only a new executive order.
- **The penalty provision applies now.** Until July 1, 2027, misuse of the privilege where an import breaches other customs law carries the subsection (a) civil penalty. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-05" />
- **Landed cost needs the full duty stack.** See [the 2026 tariff stack guide](/guides/tariff-stack-2026/) for how general, additional and user-fee charges add up on one entry.

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical)">
Assume a seller's imports produce three separate violations under the interim penalty provision before July 1, 2027. The statutory ceiling is up to $5,000 for the first and up to $10,000 for each of the next two: $5,000 + (2 x $10,000) = $25,000 maximum. <C id="section-321-repeal-2027-05" /> These are caps, not fixed amounts.
</Callout>

## What to check yourself

1. Open Public Law 119-21 on govinfo.gov (PLAW-119publ21) and read section 70531 in full, including subsections (a) and (b).
2. Read CBP Dec. 26-12 on federalregister.gov (June 24, 2026, docket USCBP-2026-0760) for CBP's statement of the July 1, 2027 termination and the current suspension text.
3. Search the Federal Register API for "de minimis" after November 8, 2026 for any CBP rule implementing the statutory change.
4. Follow docket USCBP-2026-0298 ("Low-Value Shipments") for the final rule and its effective date.
5. If you sell gifts or other non-commercial imports, get advice on how the amended statute applies to them before July 1, 2027.
