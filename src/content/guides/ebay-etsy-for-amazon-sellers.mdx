eBay charges 13.6% on the item, the shipping and the sales tax, plus $0.40 an order. Every other number you have read for eBay's fee is that one with a piece missing. The [second marketplace guide](/guides/second-marketplace-decision/) puts Walmart first for a US physical-goods seller. These two are the answer when your product does not fit that pattern — and both are frequently misread from their headline rates.

## eBay, and why the number moves

| Source | Reported final value fee |
| --- | --- |
| SPX Commerce | 12.9% + $0.30 (including payment processing) |
| ProfitCalcu | 13.25% + $0.30, capped at $750 per item |
| Platform Fee Calculator | 13.35% + $0.40 on item plus shipping, processing included |
| Taxomate | 13.6% + $0.40 |
| EcomCalcTools | 14.0% effective on a $100 sale, most categories, no store |
| Webgility | 12%–15% plus a small per-order charge |
| eBay's own stated range (per one source) | 2.5%–15.3% by category |

**These are largely the same fee described differently.** Whether the per-order charge is folded in, whether the base includes shipping, and which category — those three choices produce the spread.

**Two things every source agrees on:**

**Payment processing is included.** No separate charge, unlike Etsy.

**The fee is charged on item plus shipping.** One source's example: a $20 item with $8 shipping is charged on $28. **That inverts the usual instinct to move cost into shipping** — on eBay it changes nothing except your fee base.

One 2026 change: clothing and accessories rose from 15% to 15.3%, with the per-order charge unchanged.

**Listing fees:** at up to 250 free fixed-price listings a month, then $0.35 each. Store subscribers get more — one source says up to 10,000 depending on plan.

**The store subscription** will reduce final value fees, with one source citing as little as 4.6% on qualifying categories. Reported break-even logic: worth it if you regularly exceed the free listing allowance, or if the reduced rates on your volume exceed the subscription cost.

## Etsy, and the fee nobody models

**The four-part stack:**

| Component | Reported |
| --- | --- |
| Transaction fee | 6.5% of item price including shipping |
| Payment processing | ~3% + $0.25 |
| Listing fee | $0.20, renewed every four months |
| Offsite ads | 15%, mandatory above a revenue threshold |

**Total effective rate without offsite ads:** at roughly 9.5% to 11.3%.

**The offsite ads program is the decision point.** One source reports it as mandatory for sellers above $10,000 in annual revenue and as significantly increasing costs. Another frames the comparison directly: for items priced $20 to $200 **without** offsite ads, Etsy is typically cheaper than eBay by about 3.5 to 4 percentage points — and **if 40% or more of your Etsy sales trigger the 15% fee, eBay's flat rate may be predictably cheaper.**

That is the sharpest published framing of the choice, and it turns on a number you can only measure after selling there for a while.

## Which fits which product

Reported guidance, and it is consistent:

**eBay wins on:**
- **Used, refurbished and non-handmade goods** — no category restrictions
- **High-value items above $500** with a store subscription
- **Parts, accessories and collectibles** where search demand is specific
- **International buyers**, via the global shipping program
- Cases where Etsy's offsite ads attribution is heavy

**Etsy wins on:**
- **Handmade, craft, custom and vintage** items — its buyer base actively searches for these
- **Items $20 to $200** where offsite ads do not trigger
- Products where the audience justifies a premium

**Etsy's handmade and vintage policy** is the hard constraint: some items simply do not belong there. **For a standard private-label FBA product, that usually settles it.**

## What this means for an FBA seller

**Neither is a general-purpose second marketplace.** Walmart is, for the reasons in the [Walmart guide](/guides/walmart-wfs/) — same logistics, same customer expectation, comparable fulfillment program.

**eBay is worth considering** if you have returned, open-box or refurbished inventory that Amazon will not let you list as new, or if you sell parts and accessories where eBay's search behavior differs. It is also a route for [removal-order inventory](/guides/removal-vs-hold-decision/) that would otherwise be liquidated at a fraction of value.

**Etsy is worth considering** only if your product genuinely fits the handmade, custom or vintage policy. Listing a mass-produced private-label item there is a policy problem waiting to happen.

**Both add the same overheads as any channel:** sales tax on direct-adjacent sales — see the [sales tax guide](/guides/sales-tax-nexus/) — pricing constraints from [parity policies](/guides/cross-channel-price-parity/), and multi-channel inventory and accounting.

**And on fulfillment:** [MCF](/guides/mcf-off-amazon-orders/) can ship eBay orders from your FBA inventory, with the caveat that alternative last-mile providers being required on eBay because Amazon's in-house delivery cannot be used there.

## What to do

**Compare on your own category and price point**, not on headline rates. Every source's number is correct for some configuration.

**Remember eBay charges on item plus shipping.** Free shipping is not free; it moves cost into the fee base.

**Measure Etsy's offsite ads attribution before judging.** It is the line that decides the comparison and you cannot know it in advance.

**Check the store subscription break-even** if you list more than 250 items a month or sell high-ticket goods.

**Treat these as product-fit decisions, not diversification.** The diversification argument is in the [second marketplace guide](/guides/second-marketplace-decision/); these two are answers to "where does this specific product belong."

**Run it on your own figures.** The [margin and break-even calculator](/tools/margin-calculator/) takes your price, cost of goods, referral rate, fulfillment fee, returns and ad spend and returns net profit per unit, net margin and the maximum ACOS the product can carry. Nothing is stored and nothing leaves your browser.
