Since January 1, 2026 nobody at Amazon will put a label on your product. This is the 2026 change that received the least coverage relative to its effect. It is not a fee increase, so it does not appear in rate cards. It is a service withdrawal, and the cost lands wherever you put it.

## What happened

as of **January 1, 2026, Amazon no longer offers FBA prep and labeling services in the US.**

The work that service covered:

- **FNSKU labeling** — the barcode identifying your unit
- **Poly-bagging** — with suffocation warnings where required
- **Bubble wrapping and protective packaging**
- **Bundling and set preparation**
- **Taping, opacity bagging** and other category-specific requirements

None of that became unnecessary. FBA still requires it. Amazon simply stopped being the one who does it.

## The three routes, and what each one actually costs you

### Route 1: the supplier preps it

**Cost:** negotiated into the unit price, frequently at a lower rate than a domestic prep center.

**The risk:** this as risky specifically because FBA compliance errors are costly. A factory in another country, working from written instructions, applying US-market labeling requirements they do not otherwise deal with, at scale, with no ability to check the result before it ships.

**When it works:** simple prep — a poly bag and a label — on a product you have shipped repeatedly with the same supplier. **When it fails:** the failure is discovered at the fulfillment center, on an entire container, after freight and duty have been paid.

### Route 2: a prep center

**Cost:** at **$0.35 to $2.00 per unit** depending on the work. Prep-center price lists give $0.50 to $2.00 and $0.35 to $1.50.

**What you get:** the goods arrive as they come from the manufacturer and leave FBA-ready. this as the structural advantage of a 3PL over AWD in 2026 — prep is built in rather than needing a separate step.

**What to check before signing:** per-unit rates by prep type, receiving fees, storage while prep happens, minimum volumes, turnaround time, and what happens when they make a mistake. That last one is the question most sellers do not ask.

### Route 3: in-house

**Cost:** your time, materials and space.

**When it makes sense:** low volume, high margin, simple prep, or products where you want to inspect every unit anyway.

**When it stops making sense:** almost always sooner than sellers think. Prep is repetitive work with no learning curve after the first hundred units, and the hours it consumes are hours not spent on sourcing or listings.

## The cost of getting it wrong

Amazon's 2026 fee changes fold late, missing and misrouted shipments into **a single inbound defect fee averaging $0.60 per unit**. The $0.32–$5.72 range quoted in some 2026 articles is a vendor reading of the old per-defect schedule and does not appear on Amazon's summary; plan on the $0.60 average and read the exact line on the Send to Amazon screen. Triggers include mislabeled shipments, delivery to the wrong warehouse, deleted shipment plans, abandoned inventory beyond 30 days and non-compliance with FBA prep requirements.

That is roughly an eighty-fold increase at the top of the range, and it is the enforcement mechanism for the change described above. Amazon stopped doing the work and made the penalty for doing it badly considerably larger in the same year.

## Why this broke the upstream storage maths

This is the connection most coverage misses, and it matters if you are evaluating where to hold inventory.

Amazon Warehousing and Distribution stores bulk inventory upstream of FBA at lower rates. But **AWD does not prep.** Reporting is explicit: if you use AWD, your products must arrive already prepped for FBA — which means a separate prep step somewhere, at an additional reported **$0.50 to $1.50 or more per unit.**

A 3PL reserve warehouse, by contrast, typically includes prep in its service. So the honest comparison stopped being "AWD storage against 3PL storage" and became **"AWD storage plus prep somewhere else" against "3PL storage with prep built in."**

Our [warehousing guide](/guides/awd-vs-3pl-warehousing/) works through what that does to the numbers. The short version: it narrows AWD's storage advantage considerably.

## What to do

**Find out who is prepping your inventory right now.** If you have not addressed this since December, either your supplier is doing it, your 3PL is doing it, or your shipments are being flagged. All three are worth knowing about.

**Get prep quoted per SKU, not per shipment.** Prep cost varies enormously by product — a poly bag and a label against a bundle requiring assembly, opacity bagging and a set label. A blended rate hides which SKUs are expensive.

**Put the number in your unit economics.** Between $0.35 and $2.00 per unit is not a rounding error against a fulfillment fee of a few dollars. It belongs in the [margin calculator](/tools/margin-calculator/) as a line, and it belongs in [product research](/guides/product-research-cost-errors/) before you source.

**If the supplier preps, verify a sample before the full run.** Ask for photographs of the prepped unit, or have a prep center or inspection service check the first shipment. The cost of checking is a fraction of the cost of a rejected container.

**Ask a prospective prep center what happens when they mislabel a unit.** Whether they cover the inbound defect fee tells you what kind of partner they are.

**Reconsider bundles.** Bundling raises the unit price — which now moves the [fulfillment fee price band](/guides/price-tier-repricing-trap/) — and raises prep cost, because assembly is charged accordingly. Both need checking together.

**Check it on your own package.** The [dimensional weight calculator](/tools/dimensional-weight/) takes your packed dimensions and weight and returns billable weight and cubic feet — the two figures that drive freight and storage. Nothing is stored and nothing leaves your browser.
