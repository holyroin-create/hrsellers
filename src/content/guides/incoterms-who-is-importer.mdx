A supplier's price quote, whether it says DDP, FOB or something else, is a term of your purchase contract. The customs side runs on a different question: whose name goes on the entry as the importer, because that party owes the duty. CBP's regulations define the importer as "the person primarily liable for the payment of any duties on the merchandise," or an authorized agent, and list who that can be: the consignee, the importer of record, the actual owner if an actual owner's declaration and superseding bond is filed, or a transferee of goods in a bonded warehouse. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-01" />

That liability is "a personal debt due from the importer to the United States," and payment to a broker covering duties does not relieve the importer of liability if the broker does not pay them. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-02" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-03" /> So when a supplier offers to handle import, the practical question is whether the supplier will be named as importer of record, and if so, whether that supplier can lawfully act as one. From January 5, 2027, that second question has a new answer for foreign companies. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-09" />

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page covers who may act as importer of record under the regulations. Which party takes the role on a given shipment is something to settle in your sales contract and confirm with your broker. The trade terms themselves (Incoterms rules) are not summarized here; read the terms in your own contract.
</Callout>

## Who may make entry

The right to make entry comes from the shipping documents and the parties' roles, as set out in 19 CFR Part 141, Subpart B:

- **Goods arriving by common carrier.** Evidence of the right to make entry includes a properly endorsed bill of lading or air waybill, or a carrier's certificate that the named consignee "is the owner or consignee of such articles." <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-04" />
- **Goods arriving other than by common carrier.** Possession of the merchandise at the time of arrival in the United States is deemed sufficient evidence of the right to make entry. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-05" />
- **Informal entries.** CBP's October 2026 proposed rule describes current informal filing as limited to parties with the right to make entry, "i.e., an owner or purchaser of the shipment." <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-06" />

When the consignee named on the entry is not the real owner, 19 CFR 141.20 lets the consignee declare that it is not the actual owner, with the actual owner acknowledging that it will pay all additional and increased duties. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-07" />

## Nonresident corporations today

A nonresident corporation, meaning one not incorporated within the U.S. customs territory or the U.S. Virgin Islands, may not enter merchandise for consumption unless it has a resident agent authorized to accept service of process in the state where the port of entry is located, and files a bond on CBP Form 301 with the 19 CFR 113.62 conditions "having a resident corporate surety" to secure any increased and additional duties. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-08" /> Bond mechanics are covered in [Customs bonds](/guides/customs-bonds/).

## The 2027 rule on foreign importers of record

On November 6, 2026, CBP published "Heightened Requirements for Foreign Importers of Record" as an interim final rule with a request for comments. It takes effect on January 5, 2027, and comments must be received on or before January 5, 2027. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-09" /> Licensed customs brokers may request delayed compliance with the new broker rules in 19 CFR 111.29 before January 5, 2027, and all customs brokers are expected to be in full compliance no later than May 6, 2027. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-10" /> The rule is 91 FR 69977, docket USCBP-2026-1387, amends 19 CFR parts 110, 111, 141, 142 and 171, <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-11" /> and was issued under Section 2 of Executive Order 14411, "Strengthening Customs Enforcement." <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-20" />

### Who is a U.S. importer of record

New 19 CFR 110.2 sets the criteria. An individual qualifies as a U.S. importer of record if a United States citizen or lawful permanent resident. An entity must be located in the United States and organized under U.S. law, and must also meet an ownership or property test: at least 51% of its beneficial owners are U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents with control of the entity, or it maintains direct ownership of a significant amount of unencumbered real property in the United States. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-13" /> Any importer of record that does not meet those criteria is a foreign importer of record. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-12" />

| Requirement from Jan 5, 2027 | U.S. importer of record | Foreign importer of record |
|---|---|---|
| CTPAT validation | Not required | Required to enter on its own or designate a broker (otherwise see next row) |
| Without CTPAT | Not applicable | Must designate a CTPAT-validated licensed customs broker |
| Declaration on entry | Declares U.S. IOR status on CBP Forms 3461 and 7501 | Not applicable |
| Status decided by | Center Director during verification | Not applicable |

*Source: Federal Register, "Heightened Requirements for Foreign Importers of Record," 91 FR 69977 (Nov 6, 2026), checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-14" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-15" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-21" />

### What foreign importers of record must do

Under new 19 CFR 141.21, a foreign importer of record may not enter merchandise for consumption except under its requirements. A foreign IOR validated through the Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT) may enter merchandise itself or designate a customs broker; one that is not validated must designate a licensed customs broker that is CTPAT validated. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-14" />

### What changes for brokers and U.S. importers

- A U.S. importer of record may keep entering goods without CTPAT validation but must declare its U.S. IOR status on CBP Forms 3461 and 7501. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-15" />
- Before filing that declaration, a broker must verify the client qualifies as a U.S. importer of record, and brokers must keep verification records for five years. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-16" />
- A power of attorney is required even when the broker acts as the importer of record. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-17" />
- If an importer inaccurately declares U.S. IOR status, CBP may require entry summary at time of entry or discontinue immediate delivery, for both formal and informal entries. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-18" />

The existing nonresident-corporation rule is not replaced: CBP states that existing references to a "nonresident importer" in its regulations are not in conflict with the new rule. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-19" />

## What this means for a DDP or FOB quote

Read a supplier's offer against the rules above by asking one question first: who will be importer of record?

- **If the supplier will be importer of record**, it owes the duty as the importer, and from January 5, 2027, a foreign supplier needs CTPAT validation or a CTPAT-validated licensed broker to enter goods for consumption. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-14" /> A nonresident corporation also needs a resident agent and a bond with a resident corporate surety. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-08" />
- **If you will be importer of record**, the duty is your personal debt, and paying a broker does not discharge it if the broker does not pay the duties. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-02" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-03" /> From January 5, 2027, your U.S. IOR status must be declared on the entry and entry summary, and a broker filing that declaration for you must first verify that you qualify. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-15" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-16" /> A broker granted delayed compliance has until no later than May 6, 2027 to comply fully. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-10" />
- **If you are named as consignee but are not the owner**, the actual owner's declaration under 19 CFR 141.20 is the mechanism that shifts additional duty to the actual owner. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-07" />

Valuation questions that depend on which sale is used, such as first-sale pricing, are in [First-sale valuation](/guides/first-sale-valuation/).

## Decision table: which importer-of-record rules apply to you

From January 5, 2027, the first question on any entry is which importer-of-record category the filer falls into. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-09" /> The table maps common seller setups to the category and to what the rules then require.

| If your situation is… | Category | What the rules require |
|---|---|---|
| You import in your own name as an individual U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident | U.S. IOR | Declare U.S. IOR status on CBP Forms 3461 and 7501; CTPAT validation not required <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-13" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-15" /> |
| Your company is located and organized in the U.S. and controlled by 51%+ U.S.-citizen or LPR beneficial owners | U.S. IOR | Same declaration; your broker verifies status first and keeps the records five years <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-13" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-16" /> |
| Your company is located and organized in the U.S. but meets neither the ownership test nor the real property test | Foreign IOR | CTPAT validation to enter yourself, or a CTPAT-validated licensed broker <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-12" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-13" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-14" /> |
| Your overseas supplier acts as importer of record | Foreign IOR | Same CTPAT route; a nonresident corporation also needs a resident agent and a Form 301 bond with a resident corporate surety <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-14" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-08" /> |
| You are named consignee but are not the actual owner | Consignee | The actual owner's declaration under 19 CFR 141.20 <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-07" /> |
| Your broker acts as importer of record for you | Broker as IOR | A power of attorney is still required <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-17" /> |
| A U.S. IOR declaration turns out to be inaccurate | Enforcement | CBP may require entry summary at time of entry or discontinue immediate delivery <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-18" /> |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register, CBP interim final rule 91 FR 69977, and eCFR, 19 CFR Part 141, Subpart B, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-11" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-08" /> <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-07" />

The third row is the one most likely to surprise a seller. Being located and organized in the U.S. is only part of the test: the entity also needs either qualifying ownership in control or significant unencumbered U.S. real property, <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-13" /> and an importer of record that falls short is a foreign IOR by definition. <C id="incoterms-who-is-importer-12" /> Check your ownership against the criteria before a broker asks you to sign the declaration.
