Every product you import gets a number from the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS), and that number sets the duty rate. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) is the agency solely authorized to interpret the HTS and to issue legally binding rulings on the tariff classification of imports. <C id="hts-classification-basics-01" /> The U.S. International Trade Commission (USITC) publishes and maintains the schedule and can provide technical information on its structure and modification, but it does not decide your classification. <C id="hts-classification-basics-02" />

The practical answer for a seller: the legal text of the HTS ends at the 8-digit level, which is where tariff rates are assigned, <C id="hts-classification-basics-03" /> and the 10-digit number (the 8-digit subheading plus a statistical suffix) is what must be used on entries. <C id="hts-classification-basics-04" /> You arrive at that number by applying the General Rules of Interpretation and the notes in the schedule, you are expected to use reasonable care doing it, and if you want certainty before you ship, you can ask CBP for a binding ruling.

This page explains how classification works. The correct code for your product is your responsibility as importer; a CBP binding ruling is the formal way to confirm it. Which additional duties stack on top of the base rate is covered in [Tariff stack 2026](/guides/tariff-stack-2026/).
</Callout>

## How an HTS number is built

The HTS is built on the Harmonized System (HS) nomenclature promulgated by the World Customs Organization, which is the shared international basis for the U.S. schedules. <C id="hts-classification-basics-07" /> The first six digits of a U.S. HTS import number are the same as the first six of a Schedule B export number; the two diverge at the 8- and 10-digit levels. <C id="hts-classification-basics-07" />

| Level | What it is called | Legal status |
|---|---|---|
| 4 digits | Heading | Part of the legal text |
| 6 digits | Subheading (international HS level) | Part of the legal text |
| 8 digits | Subheading where tariff rates are assigned | End of the legal text |
| 10 digits | 8-digit subheading plus statistical suffix | Number that must be used on entries |

*Source: USITC, HTS External User Guide and Harmonized Tariff Information FAQ, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="hts-classification-basics-05" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-03" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-04" />

The USITC user guide calls the 4-digit categories "headings" and treats the 6-, 8- and 10-digit classifications as subheadings. <C id="hts-classification-basics-05" /> Each line carries rates in three columns: General Rate of Duty, Special Rate of Duty and Column 2 Rate of Duty. <C id="hts-classification-basics-06" /> Choosing the column for your goods sits outside this page.

The 8-digit number also appears on paperwork you control. The commercial invoice requirements in 19 CFR 142.6 list "the appropriate eight-digit subheading" from the HTSUS among the invoice contents. <C id="hts-classification-basics-30" />

## The rules that decide the number

Classification is not a keyword search. The USITC states that the HTS classifies products using the General Rules of Interpretation (GRI), alongside Additional U.S. Rules and the section and chapter notes. <C id="hts-classification-basics-08" /> The GRIs and the Additional U.S. Rules are applied to the nomenclature in chapters 1 through 97. <C id="hts-classification-basics-09" />

Three kinds of text carry legal weight and one does not:

- **General Notes.** The HTS General Notes section contains the general notes, the General Rules of Interpretation and the general statistical notes. <C id="hts-classification-basics-12" />
- **Section and chapter notes.** Chapter notes are "internationally prescribed legal language under the HS," and chapters may also carry additional U.S. notes, heading notes or subheading notes. <C id="hts-classification-basics-10" />
- **The heading and subheading text itself.**
- **Footnotes do not count.** The USITC guide states that footnotes are merely informational and their presence or absence has no legal effect. <C id="hts-classification-basics-11" />

One tool limitation matters here: the HTS search on hts.usitc.gov does not search chapter or section notes. <C id="hts-classification-basics-13" /> A keyword hit on a heading is a starting point; the notes for that section and chapter can still move the product elsewhere, so read them directly.

The schedule also changes during the year. The USITC announced that 2026 HTS Revision 22 was published on October 30, 2026, <C id="hts-classification-basics-14" /> and each edition includes a change record listing major changes since the previous edition. <C id="hts-classification-basics-15" /> A number that was right at your last reorder is worth rechecking against the current revision.

## Reasonable care: the importer's side of the bargain

Classification responsibility sits with the importer. The USITC says importers are ultimately responsible for knowing CBP requirements, <C id="hts-classification-basics-32" /> and CBP's penalty guidelines in Appendix B to 19 CFR Part 171 set the standard: importers must exercise reasonable care in fulfilling their responsibilities involving entry, including providing a classification and value for the merchandise. <C id="hts-classification-basics-16" />

The same appendix gives concrete markers:

- CBP may consider an importer's failure to follow a binding CBP ruling a lack of reasonable care. <C id="hts-classification-basics-17" />
- It gives as an example of an unreasonable classification imported snow skis classified as water skis. <C id="hts-classification-basics-18" />
- Negligence is defined as a failure to exercise the degree of reasonable care and competence expected from a person in the same circumstances. <C id="hts-classification-basics-19" />

For a marketplace seller, the takeaway is procedural: keep the product facts, the notes you read and any rulings you relied on, so you can show how you got to the number.

## Binding rulings under 19 CFR Part 177

If a classification is uncertain or the duty difference is large, Part 177 lets you get CBP's position in writing before you import.

**What a ruling is.** A ruling is a written statement from CBP Headquarters or the appropriate CBP office that interprets the law as it applies to a specific set of facts. <C id="hts-classification-basics-20" />

**Who can ask.** Any person who, as an importer or exporter or otherwise, has a direct and demonstrable interest in the question may request one. <C id="hts-classification-basics-21" />

**When.** Generally, a ruling may be requested only for prospective transactions, <C id="hts-classification-basics-22" /> and no ruling letter will be issued for a completed transaction or on an issue pending before the Court of International Trade or the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. <C id="hts-classification-basics-23" />

**What to send.** Each request must contain a complete statement of all relevant facts, and classification requests should be accompanied by photographs, drawings or other pictorial representations of the article. <C id="hts-classification-basics-24" /> The request must also say whether the same transaction is being considered by any CBP office or whether the issue is before the courts. <C id="hts-classification-basics-29" /> Tariff classification requests are addressed to the Director, National Commodity Specialist Division, in New York. <C id="hts-classification-basics-25" /> The USITC also notes you can request a binding electronic ruling from CBP. <C id="hts-classification-basics-31" />

**What it gets you.** A ruling letter represents CBP's official position on the transaction or issue described and is binding on all CBP personnel until modified or revoked. <C id="hts-classification-basics-26" /> A classification ruling applies only to transactions involving articles identical to the sample submitted or the description in the ruling. <C id="hts-classification-basics-27" /> Change the material, the components or the retail set, and you are outside the ruling.

**How it can change.** For a ruling in effect 60 days or more, CBP publishes a proposed modification or revocation in the Customs Bulletin with 30 calendar days for comment, and a final change applies to merchandise entered 60 calendar days after the final notice is published. <C id="hts-classification-basics-28" />

<Callout type="example" title="Where USITC help fits">
The USITC answers tariff questions, but describes that help as "not binding advice" that can assist you in talking to CBP staff. <C id="hts-classification-basics-31" /> Only a CBP ruling binds CBP.
</Callout>

| Question | Part 177 answer |
|---|---|
| Who issues the ruling | CBP Headquarters or the appropriate CBP office |
| Can I ask after the goods arrived | No ruling for a completed transaction |
| Does it cover my next product | Only articles identical to the sample or description |
| Can it be withdrawn | Yes, by modification or revocation after notice and comment |

*Source: eCFR, 19 CFR Part 177 Subpart A, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="hts-classification-basics-20" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-23" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-27" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-28" />

## Common classification mistakes and what the rules say

Each mistake below is paired with the rule it runs into and the consequence that follows from that rule.

| Mistake | What the rule actually says | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Using the supplier's 6-digit HS code on the entry | HTS and Schedule B numbers share only the first six digits; the 10-digit number must be used on entries <C id="hts-classification-basics-07" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-04" /> | Six digits cannot show the rate, which is assigned at the 8-digit level <C id="hts-classification-basics-03" /> |
| Taking the duty treatment from a footnote | Footnotes are merely informational and have no legal effect <C id="hts-classification-basics-11" /> | The legal text and notes decide, not the footnote |
| Relying on the HTS search box to find notes | The search does not search chapter or section notes <C id="hts-classification-basics-13" /> | You can miss notes that, with the GRIs, decide the number <C id="hts-classification-basics-08" /> |
| Reusing a ruling for a modified product | A ruling covers only articles identical to the sample or description <C id="hts-classification-basics-27" /> | The new variant has no ruling behind it |
| Requesting a ruling for a transaction that is already completed | No ruling letter is issued for a completed transaction <C id="hts-classification-basics-23" /> | Ask before the transaction is completed |
| Treating USITC answers as binding | USITC help is not binding advice; CBP alone issues binding rulings <C id="hts-classification-basics-31" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-01" /> | Only a CBP ruling binds CBP personnel <C id="hts-classification-basics-26" /> |
| Ignoring a ruling that went against you | CBP may treat failure to follow a binding ruling as a lack of reasonable care <C id="hts-classification-basics-17" /> | Exposure under the penalty guidelines' reasonable care standard <C id="hts-classification-basics-16" /> |
| Classifying from last year's schedule | Each edition carries a change record; 2026 Revision 22 was published October 30, 2026 <C id="hts-classification-basics-15" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-14" /> | A changed number can go unnoticed |

*Source: U.S. International Trade Commission, HTS FAQ and user guide, and eCFR, 19 CFR Parts 171 and 177, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="hts-classification-basics-04" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-11" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-17" /> <C id="hts-classification-basics-27" />

The first and last rows are the cheapest to prevent: request the full 10-digit number from whoever classifies, and compare it against the current edition's change record when a new revision is published.
