The [supplier verification guide](/guides/supplier-verification/) covers establishing that a factory exists. This covers establishing that what it made is what you ordered — and it is the cheapest insurance in the entire import process.

## The three inspection points

**Factory audit** — before you order. Verifies the facility, equipment, capacity and systems. Reported at **$100 to $300 per man-day** for basic on-site verification, or **$800 to $2,000** for a full audit by a firm such as SGS or TÜV. One provider is reported at $440 per man-day for audits typically requiring one to two days.

**During-production inspection (DUPRO)** — mid-run. Catches a systematic defect while there is still production left to correct it. The one most sellers skip and the one that saves the most on a first order.

**Pre-shipment inspection (PSI)** — when production is substantially complete and goods are packed. The final check before release.

## Why PSI timing is the point

A pre-shipment inspection happens at the moment when **the supplier still has the goods and you still have the balance payment.**

That is the only point in the transaction where refusing costs you nothing but time. After the goods ship, refusing means a refund claim against a company in another jurisdiction, and after they arrive it means a return, a destruction order or selling defective stock.

**This is also why the inspection must be independent.** Reporting is direct that the inspector is almost always a third-party firm you hire, not the factory's own people — and one source recommends commissioning one even where the supplier provides its own QC documentation.

**The threshold to work from:** one source puts it at any order above **$10,000 in product value**, and describes the cost as negligible relative to a defective shipment clearing customs.

## AQL, and what it does not do

AQL — Acceptance Quality Limit — is a sampling standard, described in ISO 2859-1, specifying how a sample size and an acceptance number are derived from a lot size and a chosen quality level.

**It is a tool, and reporting is careful about its limits:** AQL assumes your process is under control. Many factories run mixed lines and mixed operators, which is precisely the condition under which a sample stops representing the lot.

**When full inspection is warranted** rather than sampling, one source gives the test plainly: if the money at risk on returns, chargebacks or brand damage exceeds the inspection and rework bill, you are in full-inspection territory. That is most often true on **first shipments** — when the supplier relationship is unproven, the specification is untested, and the downside is a listing that has not built any resilience yet.

**Note the pricing implication:** one provider is reported at $150 per man-day covering both AQL sampling and 100% full inspection, which makes the choice between them less costly than sellers assume.

## What to have inspected

Most inspection checklists cover function and cosmetics. For a marketplace seller, four additional items belong on it — and each one prevents a problem this site covers elsewhere:

**FNSKU labels and placement.** Applied, scannable, and covering the manufacturer barcode entirely — see the [barcodes guide](/guides/gs1-barcodes-fnsku/). A labelling error discovered at the fulfilment centre is an inbound defect fee.

**Country of origin marking.** Present, permanent, conspicuous — see the [marking guide](/guides/origin-marking-requirements/). Unmarked goods are reported to carry a 10% marking duty.

**Poly bags and suffocation warnings**, where required.

**Packaged dimensions and weight.** Measured, not assumed. They set your size tier and every layer of the [storage bill](/guides/fba-storage-four-layers/), and a carton 2cm larger than specified can move a tier.

**Container loading supervision** is a separate service worth knowing about — verifying loading, quantity, packaging and condition at the point the container is stuffed.

## The alignment problem

One reported failure mode is worth isolating because it is procedural rather than technical: **not aligning all three parties — you, the supplier and the inspector — on quantities, tolerances and criteria.**

An inspection against a specification the factory never agreed to produces a dispute rather than a decision. Reporting's recommendation is to combine inspection with **clear tolerances and photographs**, agreed in advance.

**The practical version:** your purchase order should state the AQL level, the defect classification, the tolerances, and who bears the cost of rework or re-inspection. If that is not in the order, the inspection report is an opinion.

## What to ask an inspection provider

Drawn from what the source material treats as differentiating:

- **Is the rate all-inclusive?** Reporting notes "all-inclusive" often is not, with travel surcharges appearing separately.
- **What is the reporting turnaround?** Same-day and real-time platforms are reported; a PDF the following morning is slower than the decision window sometimes allows.
- **Do reports include photographs and measurement notes?** Reporting names defect photos and measurements as the part of the report that actually decides go or no-go.
- **Can you supervise container loading?**
- **What happens if the factory refuses access?** It happens, and how the provider handles it tells you what you are buying.

**And one structural caution:** where a sourcing agent runs QC as part of their standard service, the inspection is built in — but it is also not independent of the party who selected the factory. See the [sourcing agents guide](/guides/sourcing-agents/).

## What to do

**Inspect the first order, always.** The supplier relationship is unproven and the specification is untested.

**Use the $10,000 threshold** for subsequent orders unless you have reason to inspect more often.

**Add a DUPRO on any first production run**, not just a PSI. Finding a systematic defect at 80% complete is better than at 100%.

**Put the AQL level, tolerances and rework terms in the purchase order.**

**Add the four marketplace-specific checks** — labels, marking, poly bags, dimensions.

**Never release the balance payment before the report.** The sequence is the whole point: inspection, then payment, then shipment.
