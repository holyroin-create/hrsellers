A QC inspection is a commercial step you arrange with your supplier. What makes it worth paying for is that several things you are legally responsible for depend on what leaves the factory. If your product is under a CPSC rule and made outside the United States, the importer is generally the finished product certifier that must issue the certificate. <C id="qc-inspections-01"/> You may rely on another party's work, but you remain legally responsible for what the certificate says. <C id="qc-inspections-02"/> <C id="qc-inspections-03"/> An inspection is how you find out, before you pay the balance, whether the goods match what you certified, ordered and will declare.

## The CPSC side: inspections and testing are different jobs

Certificate rules are covered in [CPSC certificates and testing](/guides/us-product-compliance/); this section only covers how factory QC fits around them. Part 1110 itself does not address the type or frequency of testing needed to support a certificate, <C id="qc-inspections-05"/> and a finished product certificate must accompany each finished product or finished product shipment. <C id="qc-inspections-04"/>

For children's products, all manufacturers must conduct periodic testing. <C id="qc-inspections-06"/> One option CPSC describes is a production testing plan. Such a plan describes the production management techniques and tests that must be performed to provide a high degree of assurance that products made after certification continue to comply, <C id="qc-inspections-07"/> and may include recurring testing or process management techniques such as control charts and statistical process control programs, <C id="qc-inspections-08"/> or failure modes and effects analyses designed to control potential variations in product manufacturing. <C id="qc-inspections-09"/> The test methods used need not be the ones used for certification. <C id="qc-inspections-10"/>

Samples sent to the lab for periodic testing must be representative, <C id="qc-inspections-11"/> and the selection procedure must provide a basis for inferring compliance about the population of untested products. <C id="qc-inspections-12"/> For certification testing, manufacturers must submit samples of the children's product or samples identical in all material respects to it. <C id="qc-inspections-23"/> An inspection that compares bulk production against the approved sample is how you learn whether that is still true.

### Material changes

A material change is a change in the product's design, manufacturing process or sourcing of component parts that a manufacturer exercising due care knows, or should know, could affect compliance, <C id="qc-inspections-13"/> and after one the manufacturer must have the product tested and issue a new Children's Product Certificate. <C id="qc-inspections-14"/> A sourcing change is material when replacing one component part with another could affect compliance with the applicable rule. <C id="qc-inspections-15"/> Checking materials against the approved bill of materials is how an inspection can surface such a change. The required records, including periodic testing records, must be kept for five years. <C id="qc-inspections-16"/>

## The payment side: put the inspection where the money is

Under a letter of credit, no payment obligation arises until the goods have been shipped as promised. <C id="qc-inspections-21"/> Ask your bank whether an inspection report can be one of the LC's required documents, which ITA notes are detailed and prone to errors and discrepancies. <C id="qc-inspections-22"/> With deposit-and-balance terms, the equivalent is to make the balance payable only after an inspection report you accept. Payment options are compared in [supplier payment terms](/guides/supplier-payment-terms/).

## The customs side: discounts and damage

Settle defects before the goods ship. Any rebate of, or other decrease in, the price made after the date of importation is disregarded in determining transaction value. <C id="qc-inspections-18"/> A credit note you negotiate after the goods land lowers what you pay the supplier, not the value you declared.

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical)">
Inputs: 2,000 units at $10.00 each ($20,000); 10% duty; 5% of units defective.
Defects found at a pre-shipment inspection and the invoice re-issued at $19,000: duty $1,900.
Defects found after arrival and a $1,000 credit note issued then: the post-import rebate is disregarded, so duty stays $2,000.
</Callout>

Customs does allow for damage in one narrow case: merchandise subject to ad valorem or compound duties and found by the port director to be partially damaged at the time of importation <C id="qc-inspections-20"/> is appraised in its condition as imported, with an allowance in value to the extent of the damage. <C id="qc-inspections-19"/> That is a CBP determination about damage, not a mechanism for supplier quality claims.

The invoice side matters too: each invoice must state in adequate detail what merchandise is contained in each individual package, <C id="qc-inspections-17"/> so a carton-count and packing-list check is also an invoice-accuracy check. See [the supplier invoice](/guides/the-supplier-invoice/).

## Common QC mistakes

| Mistake | Why it matters | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating the supplier's QC report as your compliance record | The certifier remains legally responsible for the certificate's information <C id="qc-inspections-03"/> | Review the report against your certificate before issuing it |
| Inspecting only the first production run | Children's products need periodic testing, not a one-time test <C id="qc-inspections-06"/> | Inspect each order and keep lab testing on its own schedule |
| Letting the factory pick the units for lab testing | Samples must provide a basis for inferring compliance for untested products <C id="qc-inspections-12"/> | Have the inspector draw lab samples from bulk production |
| Accepting a credit note for defects found after arrival | Post-import price decreases are disregarded for customs value <C id="qc-inspections-18"/> | Find defects before shipment and re-issue the invoice |
| Not checking carton contents against the packing list | The invoice must state what each package contains <C id="qc-inspections-17"/> | Make a carton-content check part of every inspection |

*Source: eCFR — 16 CFR 1107, 16 CFR 1110, 19 CFR 141.86 and 19 CFR 152.103, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="qc-inspections-03"/>

<Callout type="note" title="Scope">
An inspection report is commercial evidence; it does not replace a CPSC-required test or certificate. Whether a change is material, or a shipment complies, is judged against the CPSC rule for your product, so confirm with an accepted lab or the CPSC when in doubt.
</Callout>

## How to confirm the rules for your product

Read 16 CFR Part 1110 (certificates) and 16 CFR Part 1107 (periodic testing, production testing plans and material change) on the eCFR. For customs value and damage allowances, read 19 CFR 152.103 and 19 CFR 158.12. For tying payment to inspection, read ITA's "Letter of Credit" page on trade.gov and discuss the document list with your bank.
