Before the de minimis suspension, most marketplace sellers never filed a formal entry and therefore never needed a bond. That changed, and the change arrived quietly inside a policy most coverage described in other terms.

## What a bond is

A financial guarantee to CBP that the duties, taxes and fees owed on your entries will be paid.

**Three parties:** you (the importer), CBP (the beneficiary), and a surety company that issues the bond.

**It protects the government, not you.** If a claim is made against the bond, the surety pays CBP and then seeks the money from you. A bond does not reduce what you owe; it guarantees that CBP receives it.

## When you need one

Reporting describes a customs bond as **mandatory for almost all formal CBP entries** — which since the de minimis suspension covers commercial shipments above the informal threshold. See the [entry types guide](/guides/entry-type-11-vs-01/).

**And on informal entries:** reporting indicates CBP **may require a bond on informal entries valued at or below $2,500.** Confirm your position with a licensed broker rather than assuming informal entry avoids the requirement.

**Separately, ISF filing on ocean shipments** carries its own bond or filing fee, reported at $25 to $75 per shipment.

## The two shapes

**Single-entry bond.** Covers one shipment. Sized against the value of that shipment plus duties and fees. Suits an occasional importer or a first shipment while a continuous bond is arranged.

**Continuous bond.** Covers a year of entries. Reported sizing is around **10% of annual duties paid**, with a minimum — one source cites $50,000 as that minimum.

**The break-even is straightforward:** if you import more than a handful of times a year, a continuous bond is usually cheaper than repeated single-entry bonds, and it removes a step from every shipment.

## Why the sizing changed

This is the part that catches sellers who already had a bond.

**Bond amount scales with duty paid.** As the [tariff stack guide](/guides/tariff-stack-2026/) covers, effective rates on many consumer categories are reported at 20% to 30% and higher — on traffic that, before the [de minimis suspension](/guides/de-minimis-suspension-2026/), often paid nothing at all.

**So a bond sized against 2024's duty spend may be insufficient for 2026's.** And an insufficient bond means entries are rejected — a problem discovered at the border rather than in a renewal notice.

**The action:** if your import duty spend has risen materially, ask your broker whether your bond amount is still adequate before your next major shipment.

## What it costs

Bond premiums are a small percentage of the bond amount and vary by surety, importer history and risk profile. Your broker or a surety agent quotes it.

**The cost that matters is not the premium.** It is:

**Underwriting time.** A bond is credit, and it is assessed. This is not same-day.

**Collateral.** Sureties may require it, particularly for new importers or where the bond amount is large relative to the business.

**Personal guarantees.** Common for smaller importers, and worth understanding before signing — the same consideration as in the [financing guide](/guides/seller-financing/).

## Where it fits in the sequence

Against the [launch sequence](/guides/launch-sequence/), this belongs with the slow-clock items rather than the shipment-day ones.

**Before your first formal entry**, not when the goods are on the water. A shipment arriving without adequate bond coverage does not clear, and the [customs holds guide](/guides/customs-holds-and-exams/) covers what waiting costs.

**Before peak.** Bond underwriting takes time and the requirement scales with duty, which is highest when your volume is highest. Sorting it in August is a different exercise from sorting it in October.

**And review it annually**, alongside the classification review in the [tariff engineering guide](/guides/tariff-engineering-design/). Both are triggered by the same thing — your duty spend changing.

## What to do

**Establish whether you have one.** Many sellers importing through a forwarder on DDP terms have never seen a bond because the forwarder's arrangement covered it — which is fine until you change forwarder or the arrangement changes. Ask.

**Ask whose bond is being used on your entries.** If it is not yours, understand what happens if that relationship ends mid-shipment.

**Compare single-entry against continuous** on your actual shipment frequency.

**Check the amount against current duty spend**, not last year's.

**Arrange it before peak**, not during it.

**Read what you are guaranteeing.** A bond is a credit instrument with recourse against you, and the terms are worth reading once rather than never.
