Duty drawback is a refund of duties you already paid on imported goods, available when those goods (or goods made from them, or qualifying substitutes) leave the United States or are destroyed under CBP supervision. The regulation defines drawback as the refund, in whole or in part, of duties, taxes and/or fees paid on imported merchandise. <C id="duty-drawback-01" /> For most claim types the refund tops out at 99 percent of what was paid. <C id="duty-drawback-04" /> <C id="duty-drawback-07" /> <C id="duty-drawback-11" /> <C id="duty-drawback-14" />

For a marketplace seller, the realistic routes are three: goods you imported and then export unused (for example, stock moved to a foreign marketplace), goods you reject because they were defective or not to specification, and retail returns you export or destroy. Manufacturing drawback exists too, but it requires that the imported merchandise be used to manufacture or produce articles in the United States. <C id="duty-drawback-04" /> The rules live in 19 CFR Part 190; this page walks through what each type requires, the clocks that run, and the paperwork.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page summarizes the drawback regulations. Whether your exports or returns qualify, and for how much, is decided on the claim itself.
</Callout>

## What drawback refunds, and what it never refunds

Drawback can return ordinary customs duties, marking duties, internal revenue taxes that attach upon importation, merchandise processing fees, and harbor maintenance taxes. <C id="duty-drawback-02" /> Antidumping and countervailing duties are not subject to drawback at all. <C id="duty-drawback-03" /> If a large share of your landed cost is AD/CVD (see [antidumping duties](/guides/antidumping-duties/)), drawback will not recover that part.

## The four drawback types a seller will meet

| Type | What qualifies | Refund cap | Time limit to export or destroy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing, direct identification | Articles made in the US using the imported merchandise | 99% of duties, taxes and fees paid | Within 5 years after importation |
| Manufacturing, substitution | Substitute merchandise in the same 8-digit HTSUS subheading | 99% of the lesser of two duty amounts | Within 5 years of importation of designated merchandise |
| Unused merchandise | Imported goods not used in the US, exported or destroyed under CBP supervision | 99% of duties, taxes and fees paid | Within 5 years of importation and before the claim is filed |
| Rejected merchandise | Goods not conforming to sample or specifications, shipped without consent, or defective at the time of importation | 99% of duties paid | Statutory 5-year period |

### Manufacturing drawback

Direct identification manufacturing drawback covers articles manufactured or produced in the United States using imported merchandise, capped at 99 percent of duties, taxes and fees paid. <C id="duty-drawback-04" /> The substitution version lets you claim on a different input, provided it is classifiable under the same 8-digit HTSUS subheading as the imported merchandise; the cap is 99 percent of the lesser of the two duty amounts. <C id="duty-drawback-05" /> Either way, the finished articles must be exported or destroyed within 5 years of importing the merchandise designated for the claim. <C id="duty-drawback-06" />

Whether relabeling, bundling or kitting counts has to be measured against the definition: manufacture or production includes processes that make merchandise into a "new and different article" with a distinctive name, character or use. <C id="duty-drawback-24" /> If your classification is borderline, start with [HTS classification basics](/guides/hts-classification-basics/).

### Unused merchandise drawback

This is the type most relevant to sellers who move inventory abroad. It applies to imported merchandise that is exported or destroyed under CBP supervision and has not been used in the United States, with the refund capped at 99 percent. <C id="duty-drawback-07" /> The merchandise must be exported or destroyed within 5 years of importation, and before the claim is filed. <C id="duty-drawback-08" /> Operations that do not amount to manufacture or production are not treated as a use of the merchandise. <C id="duty-drawback-09" />

There is also a substitution variant: you export goods that are interchangeable with what you imported. The substituted goods must be in your possession before export or destruction and generally meet the 8-digit HTSUS substitution standard. <C id="duty-drawback-10" />

### Rejected merchandise drawback

If an imported shipment turns out defective, off-spec, or was shipped without your consent, rejected merchandise drawback can apply: the grounds are non-conformity to sample or specifications, shipment without the consignee's consent, or a determination that the goods were defective as of the time of importation, and the refund is 99 percent of duties paid. <C id="duty-drawback-11" /> Where substitution is used for rejected goods, the substitute must share both the 8-digit HTSUS subheading and the same specific product identifier, such as a part number, SKU or product code. <C id="duty-drawback-12" /> Rejected goods may alternatively be claimed as unused merchandise to the extent they qualify. <C id="duty-drawback-22" />

### Returned retail merchandise

Section 190.45 addresses a situation every marketplace seller knows: customer returns. It covers imported, duty-paid goods sold at retail, returned to and accepted by the importer, and then exported or destroyed under CBP supervision; the claimant designates an entry imported within 1 year before the export or destruction date. <C id="duty-drawback-13" /> The claimant must certify that the designated and returned goods share the same 8-digit HTSUS subheading and specific product identifier (such as a SKU), and the refund is capped at 99 percent of duties paid. <C id="duty-drawback-14" />

The 1-year window is much tighter than the 5-year limits elsewhere, so returns batches need to be matched to recent entries.

## Destruction counts, but only real destruction

For drawback purposes, destruction means destroying the goods to the extent they have no commercial value. <C id="duty-drawback-23" />

## The clocks: notice, examination, filing, records

<Flow title="Drawback sequence under 19 CFR Part 190" claim="duty-drawback-15" steps={[
  {t: "Notice of Intent", d: "CBP Form 7553 at least 5 working days before export, unless waived"},
  {t: "CBP decision", d: "Examine or waive within 2 working days of the notice"},
  {t: "Export or destroy", d: "Within the 5-year window (1 year for returned retail designation)"},
  {t: "File complete claim", d: "Transmitted within 5 years of the designated import date"},
  {t: "Keep records", d: "3 years after the claim liquidates"}
]} />

- **Notice of Intent.** CBP Form 7553 must be filed at least 5 working days before the intended exportation, unless CBP approves another period or prior notice is waived. <C id="duty-drawback-15" />
- **Examination window.** CBP decides whether to examine or waive within 2 working days of receiving the notice; if it gives no timely notice, the goods may be exported without delay. <C id="duty-drawback-16" />
- **Filing deadline.** A complete claim is timely if successfully transmitted no later than 5 years after the designated merchandise was imported. <C id="duty-drawback-17" />
- **Record retention.** Drawback records must be kept for 3 years after the claims liquidate, or longer if another law requires it. <C id="duty-drawback-19" />

If CBP later denies or reduces a claim, the refusal to pay a drawback claim is a protestable decision. <C id="duty-drawback-25" /> See [prior disclosure, protests and liquidation](/guides/prior-disclosure-protests/).

## What a complete claim contains

A complete claim includes the electronic drawback entry, any applicable Form 7553 notices, the applicable import entry data, and evidence of exportation or destruction. <C id="duty-drawback-18" /> Accepted export evidence includes carrier records such as ocean bills of lading and air waybills, records from a CBP-approved electronic export system, and official postal records for mail shipments. <C id="duty-drawback-20" /> For mail or parcel post exports, official postal records describing the shipment are sufficient, provided you identify them on the drawback entry and keep them on file. <C id="duty-drawback-21" />

## What a marketplace seller can realistically use

Reading the conditions against typical seller activity:

- **Cross-border inventory transfers** of goods still in original condition fit unused merchandise drawback, as long as the goods were not used in the US and leave within 5 years. <C id="duty-drawback-08" />
- **Defective or off-spec inbound shipments** you send back to the supplier fit rejected merchandise drawback. <C id="duty-drawback-11" />
- **Customer returns** you export or destroy fit returned retail merchandise drawback only if you can match each return to an entry imported within the prior year and to the same SKU and 8-digit subheading. <C id="duty-drawback-13" /> <C id="duty-drawback-14" />
- **AD/CVD-heavy products** recover only the ordinary duties and fees, never the AD/CVD portion. <C id="duty-drawback-03" />

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical inputs)">
Suppose an importer paid $4,000 in ordinary duties and $150 in merchandise processing fees on an entry, then exports the full lot unused within the time limit. At the 99 percent cap, the maximum refund is 0.99 x $4,150 = $4,108.50. If $1,000 of the original duty had been countervailing duty, that $1,000 would be excluded first: 0.99 x $3,150 = $3,118.50.
</Callout>

Small sellers should weigh the per-claim effort, including entry matching, export proof and record retention, against the refund amount.

## What to check yourself

1. Open 19 CFR Part 190 on eCFR and read §§ 190.3, 190.31, 190.41 and 190.45 for the drawback type you plan to use.
2. Confirm the 8-digit HTSUS subheading on your import entries matches the goods you export, and that SKU-level records exist if you rely on substitution or returns.
3. Check the import date on each designated entry against the 5-year limit (or the 1-year designation window for returned retail goods).
4. Read § 190.35 before shipping to confirm the 5-working-day notice timing and whether a waiver applies to you.
5. Read § 190.72 to confirm your chosen export record (carrier document, electronic export record or postal record) is listed.
6. Diary the 3-year post-liquidation record retention period in § 190.15.
