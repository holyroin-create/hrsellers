Sixty days. That is how long you have from the delivery date to claim units that never arrived, and Amazon will not tell you which shipment is short. The [reimbursement guide](/guides/fba-reimbursement-sourcing-cost/) covers what a lost unit is worth. This covers the losses that happen before the unit ever reaches your sellable inventory — and they are the ones nobody counts.

## The three situations

inbound discrepancies into three shapes that follow the same rules:

**Short receiving.** You shipped a quantity, Amazon received fewer, and the shipment closes with a gap.

**Delivered but not fully received.** The carrier shows delivery; Amazon never checks in all the units tied to that shipment.

**Investigation completed, units still missing.** Amazon finishes its internal count and closes the shipment, and the received quantity still does not match.

**All three are your problem to notice.** One source is direct: Amazon does not proactively flag discrepancies for you.

## Where to look

**Manage FBA Shipments**, under Inventory. The **Discrepancy** column in the shipping queue is the whole check: a `0` means everything was received as expected; any other number, positive or negative, is a mismatch.

**Only Closed shipments count.** Reporting is consistent that Amazon will not investigate shipments still marked Receiving or Delivered, because the counts are not final. The reconciliation tab shows an estimated eligibility date — **do not open a case before it.**

**Then go to SKU level.** The contents view breaks expected against received per SKU rather than per shipment, which tells you which product is short and by how many. Selecting the located-units figure shows whether Amazon has already reconciled or reimbursed part of it.

**And check the reports**'s list:

| Report | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory Reconciliation | What Amazon received against what is in current inventory |
| Reimbursements | What has already been credited automatically |
| Returns | Customer returns and their disposition status |

Cross-referencing those three finds units that were received or sold but are missing without a corresponding credit.

## The claim window, which nobody agrees on

Amazon set it on September 5, 2024, and the two numbers still circulating are the before and after:

| Claim type | Window (Amazon policy) |
| --- | --- |
| Missing units from a shipment to Amazon | **15 to 60 days** from the shipment delivery date |
| Lost or damaged in a fulfillment center | 60 days from the date the adjustment appears |
| Removal order lost in transit | 15 to 75 days from removal creation |
| Customer return not received back | 60 to 120 days after the refund |

The nine-month figure in older articles was the pre-September 2024 rule. A seller still working to it will discover the change on a claim declined for lateness.

**The safe operating position** follows from the window: a **monthly** review of closed shipments, weekly for high-volume sellers. At that cadence the disagreement stops mattering, because nothing ages past either window.

Reporting on resolution time puts it at **2 to 30 days**, with complicated cases up to 60.

## What a claim needs

Reported requirements, and the second one catches people:

**Proof of what you sent.** For larger shipments, documentation showing the number of boxes and total weight at carrier pickup. Signed packing slips are named for manufacturers, with date, shipment ID, product names, quantities and your signature or stamp.

**Non-editable formats only.** PDF, JPG, JPEG, PNG or TIFF. **Excel and Word files are not accepted** — which is how a well-evidenced claim gets rejected on a technicality. You may redact pricing on invoices.

**Specifics that help the investigation**: any discrepancies you already know about, a description of your shipping boxes (color, size, distinguishing marks), product codes verified against Seller Central, and any missed prep activities.

**The route:** Seller Central → Help → Contact Us → Selling on Amazon → FBA Issue → FBA inventory reimbursement, with the ASIN, FNSKU, shipment ID and the specific discrepancy.

## What you actually get back

Since March 31, 2025, reimbursement is at **documented sourcing cost** rather than sale price — see the [reimbursement guide](/guides/fba-reimbursement-sourcing-cost/).

**Which makes this a records problem before it is a claims problem.** If you have not entered sourcing costs per SKU, Amazon estimates them from comparable products, and that estimate becomes the basis for whatever you recover.

One source's caution is worth keeping: automated credits appear in the Reimbursements report but **rarely match the unit value**. Comparing each line against your own [landed cost](/guides/landed-cost-fee-stack/) is how underpayments surface — and an automatic credit at the wrong number looks like a resolved case until you check it.

## Preventing them

Most of this belongs to work covered elsewhere on this site:

**Photograph the packed boxes and contents** before they ship. Reporting names this as critical, and it costs nothing.

**Verify labels and barcodes before shipping.** A scanning failure at receiving becomes a discrepancy, and a pre-shipment check catches it while the goods are still with the supplier.

**Keep carrier pickup documentation** showing box count and weight. It is the evidence a larger claim rests on.

**Reconcile against your own records** rather than against Amazon's version of them.

## What to do

**Set a monthly calendar entry** to review closed shipments. Weekly if your volume justifies it. This single habit is what makes the window disagreement irrelevant.

**Filter to Closed and read the Discrepancy column.** Anything other than zero is a case.

**Wait for the eligibility date** shown in the reconciliation tool. Filing early wastes the attempt.

**Prepare documents as PDF or image files**, never spreadsheets.

**Enter sourcing costs per SKU** before you need them — it decides what every claim pays.

**Check automatic credits against your own landed cost.** A credit is not proof of a correct credit.
