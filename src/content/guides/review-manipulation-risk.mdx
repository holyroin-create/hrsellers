Since October 21, 2024, review manipulation is no longer only a marketplace policy problem: it is a violation of a Federal Trade Commission trade regulation rule. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-01" /> The rule targets fake reviews and testimonials, paying for positive or negative reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled review sites posing as independent, certain review suppression tactics, and fake social media influence indicators. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-02" />

For a marketplace seller, the practical line is this: you may ask every buyer for a review, but you may not pay, reward, or pressure anyone to leave a review of a particular tone, you may not post or buy reviews that misrepresent who wrote them or what they experienced, and if you give free product in exchange for reviews, the connection has to be disclosed. The sections below map each prohibition to its paragraph in 16 CFR Part 465 and the related endorsement guides in 16 CFR Part 255.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page summarizes the federal rule text. For advice on a specific review program, an attorney familiar with FTC advertising law is the route. Marketplace review policies are separate and are not covered here.
</Callout>

## What counts as a "consumer review"

Part 465 defines a consumer review as "a consumer's evaluation, or a purported consumer's evaluation, of a product, service, or business." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-03" /> The word "purported" matters: a review written by someone pretending to be a customer is still within the definition, which is what lets the rule reach fabricated reviews.

## The prohibitions in 16 CFR Part 465, section by section

| Section | Heading | What it prohibits, in short |
|---|---|---|
| 465.2 | Fake or false consumer reviews, consumer testimonials, or celebrity testimonials | Writing, creating, selling, buying or disseminating reviews that misrepresent the reviewer or their experience |
| 465.4 | Buying positive or negative consumer reviews | Compensation or incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment |
| 465.5 | Insider consumer reviews and consumer testimonials | Undisclosed reviews by officers and managers; certain solicitations to relatives and employees |
| 465.6 | Company-controlled review websites or entities | Misrepresenting that a site or entity you control gives independent reviews |
| 465.7 | Review suppression | Threats, intimidation or false accusations against reviewers; misrepresenting that displayed reviews are the full set |
| 465.8 | Misuse of fake indicators of social media influence | Selling, distributing or buying fake followers, views and similar indicators |

*Source: Federal Trade Commission, Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 89 FR 68034 (regulatory text), checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="review-manipulation-risk-16" />

### Fake reviews (465.2)

Section 465.2(a) makes it unlawful "for a business to write, create, or sell" a consumer review, consumer testimonial, or celebrity testimonial that misrepresents the reviewer or their experience. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-04" /> The rule also reaches the buyer side: under 465.2(b), a business may not purchase such a review, or disseminate such a testimonial, where it "knew or should have known" it materially misrepresented the reviewer or the experience. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-05" /> For a seller, that means hiring a review service whose reviewers never used the product is covered even if the seller did not write a word.

Two activities are carved out of 465.2: reviews that result from generalized solicitations to purchasers, and reviews that appear on a platform because the business merely engages in consumer review hosting. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-06" /> A standard "tell us what you think" request sent to all buyers falls in the first category.

### Buying sentiment (465.4)

Section 465.4 prohibits providing "compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or conditioned expressly or by implication on," a review expressing a particular sentiment. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-07" /> Note "or by implication": an offer that says "leave a review and get a refund," sent only after a buyer has said they are happy, can carry an implied condition. The section applies to negative reviews too, so paying for negative reviews of a competitor is equally within it.

### Insider reviews (465.5)

An officer or manager may not write or create a consumer review without "a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the officer's or manager's material relationship" to the business. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-08" /> Section 465.5(c) separately addresses a business that solicits or demands reviews "from any of their immediate relatives or from any employee or agent of the business." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-09" /> If staff, family members or a sourcing agent leave reviews, treat each one as an insider review that needs a disclosure.

### Company-controlled review sites (465.6)

A business may not misrepresent "that a website, organization, or entity that it controls, owns, or operates provides independent reviews or opinions." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-10" /> A "best of" comparison site run by a brand owner that ranks its own products first is the obvious risk case.

### Review suppression (465.7)

Section 465.7(a) covers the use of "an unfounded or groundless legal threat, a physical threat, intimidation, or a public false accusation" in response to a consumer review. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-11" /> Section 465.7(b) prohibits misrepresenting that the reviews displayed are all or most of the reviews when negative reviews have been suppressed. The listed exceptions cover reviews that contain trade secrets or privileged or confidential commercial or financial information; defamatory, harassing, abusive, obscene, vulgar or sexually explicit content; another individual's personal information or likeness; discriminatory content; or clearly false or misleading content, as well as reviews the seller reasonably believes are fake and reviews wholly unrelated to the products or services offered. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-12" /> On a standalone Shopify-style store, that means a moderation policy should be written around those content grounds, not around star ratings.

### Fake social media indicators (465.8)

Section 465.8 prohibits selling or distributing "fake indicators of social media influence" that the seller knew or should have known to be fake, and covers procurement of them for commercial misrepresentation. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-13" /> Bought followers and views used to make a brand look larger than it is fall here.

## Incentivized reviews and the Part 255 endorsement guides

Part 465 does not ban giving free product to reviewers. The disclosure duty comes from the FTC's Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising, 16 CFR Part 255. Under 255.5(a), a material connection between an endorser and the advertiser "must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously," and connections include "monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-17" /> The disclosure "must clearly communicate the nature of the connection sufficiently for consumers to evaluate its significance." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-18" />

Three points in the Part 255 examples bear directly on seller programs:

- **Payment for positive reviews cannot be fixed by disclosure.** Example 9 in 255.2(e) states that such reviews "are deceptive even if the payment is disclosed." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-19" />
- **Filtering distorts.** Under 255.2(d), advertisers should not take actions that distort or misrepresent what consumers think of a product; Example 8 addresses a retailer that chose to "suppress or otherwise not publish any reviews with fewer than four stars." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-20" />
- **Star ratings can mislead.** Example 255.5(b)(6)(ii) notes that a disclosed incentive program "could still be deceptive if the solicited reviews contain star ratings" that materially inflate the product's average. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-21" />

## Penalty exposure

The FTC states that the rule "will enable the Commission to seek civil penalties against violators." <C id="review-manipulation-risk-14" /> The inflation-adjusted figure for section 5(m)(1)(A) of the FTC Act is set in 16 CFR 1.98(d) at $53,088, and the section states that its amounts apply to penalties assessed after January 17, 2025, including for violations that predated that date. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-15" /> For 2026, the FTC published a notice that its civil penalty amounts "will remain unchanged during 2026" and that it will continue to apply the 2025 levels. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-22" /> The FTC revisits these amounts periodically, so check 1.98 and the Federal Register for the figure in force on the date that matters to you.

<Checklist title="Review program checks against Part 465 and Part 255" items={[
  "Review requests go to all purchasers, not only to buyers who reported a good experience",

  "No reviews bought from services, groups or agents whose reviewers did not use the product",
  "Officers, managers, employees, agents and relatives do not review without a clear disclosure of the relationship",
  "Free-product reviewers are told to disclose the free product, and the disclosure is visible next to the review",
  "Website moderation policy removes reviews only on content grounds listed in 465.7(b), not by star rating",
  "No 'independent' review or ranking site is operated by the brand without saying so",
  "No purchased followers, likes or views on brand social accounts"

| Free product with no condition on tone | Material connection that must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously | 16 CFR 255.5(a) <C id="review-manipulation-risk-17" /> |
| The same free-product program at scale | Could still deceive if incentivized star ratings materially inflate the average | 255.5 example <C id="review-manipulation-risk-21" /> |
| Paying for positive reviews and disclosing it | Deceptive even with disclosure | 255.2(e), Example 9 <C id="review-manipulation-risk-19" /> |

## What to check yourself

1. Open the final rule at the Federal Register (document 2024-18519, 89 FR 68034) and read the regulatory text of Part 465 at the end of the document, especially 465.4 and 465.7.
2. Read 16 CFR 255.5 on eCFR for the full list of material connections and the disclosure examples, and 255.2 for the review-filtering examples.
3. Check 16 CFR 1.98 on eCFR for the current inflation-adjusted penalty amount and the date from which it applies.
4. Compare your marketplace's own review policy with the federal rule; the stricter of the two governs what you can do on that marketplace.
