The [inserts guide](/guides/product-inserts/) and the [messaging guide](/guides/buyer-seller-messaging/) cover what is permitted. This covers what happens when the system decides you crossed a line — including when you did not.

## What the policy covers

Reported prohibited activities under Amazon's customer product reviews policy, and the list is wider than most sellers assume:

- **Offering incentives for reviews** — discounts, free products, refunds, gift cards or any compensation, **even when not conditioned on the review being positive**
- **Requesting changes to existing reviews**
- **Requesting positive reviews**
- **Review gating** — asking buyers to contact you before posting a negative review
- **Reviews from connected parties**
- **Fake or purchased reviews**

**Note the second and fourth.** Asking a customer to update a review after you resolved their problem, and asking an unhappy buyer to contact you first, are both things sellers do believing them to be good service.

## The enforcement mechanism

This is the part that makes the risk different from other policy areas.

**The system flags patterns, not confirmed violations.** Reporting is explicit on this, and on the consequence: **false positives are documented and common in 2026, acknowledged by Amazon's own representatives in Seller Central forums.**

**The notice tells you nothing useful.** Reported wording is of the form that activities have been detected which may indicate attempts to manipulate reviews. Reporting states plainly: Amazon does not specify which reviews triggered the flag, which buyer-seller messages were implicated, or which third-party service created the pattern. **You must identify the trigger yourself before you can address it.**

## What can trigger it without you doing anything wrong

Reported triggers, and this list is the reason for conservatism:

**A product insert requesting reviews** — including one written before you understood the policy.

**A virtual assistant's email template mentioning feedback** — written by someone you employ, running on your account.

**A sudden organic review spike** — which is what happens when a product goes viral, wins a deal, or gets covered by a creator. **Success looks like manipulation to a pattern detector.**

**A third-party agency's connections to prohibited review services** — your agency's other clients creating a pattern that touches your account.

**Supplier packaging with review requests** — a factory reusing packaging or adding its own insert.

**Competitor reports.**

**Five of those six are things done by someone other than you.**

## Why this connects to Vine

The [Vine guide](/guides/vine-and-early-reviews/) covers the programme; here is the risk framing.

Vine is described as one of the only officially sanctioned mechanisms for early review velocity, operated by Amazon itself. **Its safety is a large part of its value** — a Vine review is Amazon's own programme producing it, and no pattern detector treats that as manipulation.

Against that, an early review spike from an unofficial route is exactly the pattern the system is built to catch. **The cost comparison is not Vine's fee against a cheaper alternative** — it is Vine's fee against the possibility of an unexplained flag on the account.

## The safest posture

One source's recommendation is the most conservative available and, given the diagnostic problem, the correct one:

**Remove review-request language from every channel except Amazon's own.** Not from inserts only — from packaging, from buyer-seller messages, from any automated template, and from anything a supplier or agency sends on your behalf.

**Then use Request a Review** — the only officially approved proactive mechanism, sending Amazon's standardised message between a reported 5 and 30 days after delivery, with content the seller cannot customise.

**The reasoning:** it eliminates the most common false-positive trigger, because there is no review-request language anywhere for an algorithm to flag. Reporting's framing is right — the safest strategy is not finding clever workarounds, it is removing the exposure.

**And build reviews as a byproduct of demand rather than as a campaign.** A campaign produces a pattern; genuine sales produce a distribution.

## If you receive a notice

The [suspension guide](/guides/account-suspension-appeal/) covers the process in full. Three points specific to this category:

**Do not respond immediately.** Reporting describes rapid appeals being flagged as low quality, and this is a category where you genuinely need time — the notice does not name the trigger.

**Audit every channel** before writing anything: inserts, packaging, buyer-seller message templates, any VA or agency activity, any third-party service you or your agency uses, and recent review velocity.

**Expect to defend a negative.** Reporting on appeals describes documentation as the determining factor. Here that means demonstrating what you do not do — which is why written policies, template archives and supplier instructions matter more in this category than in any other.

## What to do

**Audit all six trigger channels now**, before there is a notice.

**Instruct suppliers in writing** that no inserts or review language may be included, and verify at [inspection](/guides/qc-inspections/).

**Audit your VA and agency templates.** If somebody sends messages on your behalf, you own what they say.

**Ask any agency what review services they use** — for their other clients as well as yours.

**Keep the archive.** Template versions, supplier instructions, dated policies. It is what an appeal in this category is built from.

**Use Request a Review, and nothing else.**
