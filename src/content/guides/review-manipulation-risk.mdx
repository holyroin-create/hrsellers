Yes, the federal government now has a specific rule against review manipulation, and it applies to individual sellers as well as companies. The FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, took effect on October 21, 2024. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-01"/> It defines a business to include an individual who sells products or services, <C id="review-manipulation-risk-16"/> and knowing violations can draw civil penalties of up to $53,088 each. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-19"/> <C id="review-manipulation-risk-21"/>

## What counts as a "consumer review"

The rule defines a consumer review as a consumer's evaluation, or a purported consumer's evaluation, of a product, service, or business. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-17"/> The word "purported" matters: a review written by someone pretending to be a customer is still a consumer review under the rule, and that is exactly what the fake-review provisions target.

"Purchasing" a review is defined broadly. It means providing something of value — money, gift certificates, products, services, discounts, coupons, contest entries, or even another review — in exchange for a consumer review. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-06"/> A refund after the review posts, a free unit, or a swap with another seller all fit.

### Fake reviews and the "knew or should have known" standard

Section 465.2(a) prohibits a business from writing, creating, or selling a review that materially misrepresents the reviewer or their experience — including that the reviewer exists at all. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-02"/> <C id="review-manipulation-risk-03"/> Section 465.2(b) goes further: it bars buying a review that the business knew or should have known was misrepresentative. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-04"/> Paragraph (c) applies the same rule to reviews procured from the business's own officers, managers, employees, or agents, or their immediate relatives, for posting on a third-party platform or website. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-05"/>

"Should have known" is the risk for sellers who hire review services. If a vendor promises a set number of five-star reviews from accounts that have never bought your product, that is the kind of misrepresentation the rule describes.

Two carve-outs keep normal practice legal. Paragraphs (b) and (c) do not apply to reviews resulting from generalized solicitations to purchasers <C id="review-manipulation-risk-09"/> or to reviews that appear simply because the business hosts consumer reviews. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-10"/> A follow-up email asking all buyers for feedback is a generalized solicitation.

### Incentives tied to sentiment

Section 465.4 makes it a violation to provide compensation or other incentives conditioned on reviews expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-07"/> "Five stars and we'll refund you" is the textbook case. So is paying a third party to post one-star reviews on a competitor's listing.

### Insiders and family-run businesses

An officer or manager may not write a review of the business or its products without a clear and conspicuous disclosure of that relationship. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-08"/> Clear and conspicuous means easily noticeable — difficult to miss — and easily understandable by ordinary consumers. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-18"/> In a small brand where the owner, a spouse and a warehouse manager all buy the product, none of them should leave undisclosed reviews.

### Fake followers and engagement

Section 465.8 also reaches the social side of a brand. It bars buying or procuring fake indicators of social media influence that the buyer knew or should have known were fake and that materially misrepresent influence or importance for a commercial purpose. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-15"/> Those indicators are the public metrics people use to judge popularity — followers, friends, subscribers, views, likes, shares, comments and the like <C id="review-manipulation-risk-29"/> — and they count as fake when generated by bots or by accounts not tied to a real person. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-30"/>

### Suppression and selective display

Anyone using unfounded legal threats, physical threats, intimidation, or public false accusations to suppress reviews violates 465.7(a). <C id="review-manipulation-risk-12"/> A business with its own storefront also may not imply its displayed reviews represent most or all reviews submitted while it hides some based on rating or negative sentiment. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-13"/> Uniform content criteria are allowed — for example, withholding a review the seller reasonably believes is fake. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-14"/>

## Where the Endorsement Guides fit

Part 465 is a rule; the Endorsement Guides in 16 CFR Part 255 are administrative interpretations of laws the FTC enforces. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-23"/> They cover a wider set of situations — influencers, sponsored posts, free product programs — and they shape how the FTC views review programs that Part 465 does not expressly ban.

An endorsement is any advertising, marketing, or promotional message for a product that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, findings, or experiences of a party other than the sponsoring advertiser. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-24"/> When the endorser's connection to the seller is not reasonably expected by the audience and could affect how much weight they give it, the connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-25"/> Advertisers should not take actions that have the effect of distorting what consumers think of their products, <C id="review-manipulation-risk-26"/> and they can be liable for misleading statements made through endorsements. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-27"/>

The Guides include an example squarely relevant to sellers: a marketer recruits ordinary consumers to get a free product in exchange for reviews. The incentive must be disclosed, and if reviewers must be positive, a disclosure is not enough. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-28"/>

## What a violation can cost

The FTC says it can seek civil penalties for a rule violation only by showing the defendant had the knowledge the FTC Act requires. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-19"/> When the rule was published, the stated maximum was $51,744 per violation, with courts required to weigh statutory factors. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-20"/> After the January 2025 inflation adjustment, 16 CFR 1.98 sets the Section 5(m)(1)(A) maximum at $53,088, <C id="review-manipulation-risk-21"/> and in September 2026 the FTC said its civil penalty amounts will remain unchanged during 2026. <C id="review-manipulation-risk-22"/>
