Every article of foreign origin imported into the United States, or its container, must be marked in a conspicuous place with its country of origin. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-01" /> The mark has to be made "legibly, indelibly, and permanently," <C id="origin-marking-requirements-02" /> and it must show the English name of the country of origin to the ultimate purchaser in the United States. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-03" /> If you sell imported goods on Amazon, Walmart, eBay, Etsy or your own store, the person the rule protects is usually your customer.

Getting it wrong is expensive: an article that is not legally marked at importation is subject to an additional duty of 10 percent of its final appraised value, unless it is exported or destroyed under customs supervision before the entry is liquidated. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-20" /> The rest of this page walks through the regulation section by section.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page summarizes the marking regulations. Whether a particular label satisfies them can be confirmed through a CBP ruling or with a licensed customs broker. How the country itself is determined (substantial transformation, the Part 102 rules) is covered in [Country of origin and sourcing](/guides/country-of-origin-sourcing/).
</Callout>

## Who the mark is for: the ultimate purchaser

Part 134 measures every marking question against the "ultimate purchaser." The regulation defines that person as generally the last person in the United States who will receive the article in the form in which it was imported. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-04" /> When an article is to be sold at retail in its imported form, the purchaser at retail is the ultimate purchaser. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-05" />

For a marketplace seller that means the mark has to survive your own receiving, prep, storage and shipping steps and still be readable by the end customer. A mark that is only on a master carton you discard does not reach that person.

The definition shifts when an article is changed in the United States. If a U.S. manufacturer or processor turns the imported article into one with "a name, character, or use" differing from the import, that manufacturer is treated as the ultimate purchaser, and the article itself is excepted from marking. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-19" />

## How the article must be marked

Subpart E sets the method and location standards.

- **Best practice is a mark made at the factory.** The regulation states that marking requirements are best met by marking worked into the article at the time of manufacture. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-06" />
- **It must be easy to find and read.** The ultimate purchaser must be able to find the marking easily and read it without strain. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-07" />
- **It must last.** The degree of permanence should be sufficient to ensure the mark stays on in any reasonably foreseeable circumstance until the article reaches the ultimate purchaser, unless deliberately removed. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-08" />
- **Stickers and tags are acceptable if placed and attached properly.** Stickers must be affixed in a conspicuous place and securely enough that they stay on unless deliberately removed, and tags must be attached in a conspicuous place. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-09" />

### Country name wording

The marking must use the full English name of the country of origin, although abbreviations that unmistakably indicate the country, such as "Gt. Britain," are acceptable. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-10" />

Two situations need extra care:

- **A U.S. or other place name appears on the article or label.** If "USA," a U.S. city or locality name (such as a brand address), or a foreign place other than the country of origin appears and may mislead or deceive the ultimate purchaser about the actual origin, the country of origin must be marked in close proximity to that name and in at least a comparable size. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-11" /> Check this rule whenever private-label packaging prints a U.S. brand address.
- **Articles usually combined with other goods.** Where an imported article such as a label will normally be combined with another product, the marking must show that the stated origin is that of the imported article only, for example "Label made (or printed) in (name of country)." <C id="origin-marking-requirements-12" />

## Containers and repacking

Subpart C covers containers and holders.

| Situation | What Part 134 requires |
|---|---|
| The article itself is excepted from marking | The outermost container or holder in which the article ordinarily reaches the ultimate purchaser must be marked |
| A container designed for or capable of reuse | The container must be individually marked to indicate its own country of origin |
| Goods repacked into retail containers after release | The repacker must not obscure or conceal the existing marking, and the importer must notify the purchaser or transferee in writing at the time of sale |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR Part 134, Subpart C, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="origin-marking-requirements-13" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-14" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-15" />

Repacking matters for sellers who receive bulk goods and kit, bundle or re-bag them at a prep center. The new retail package is what the ultimate purchaser sees, so the origin marking has to remain visible through that step.

## Exceptions in Subpart D

Section 134.32 lists general exceptions, and Section 134.33 adds a list of named articles (the "J-List"). An exception for the article does not automatically remove the container requirement described above.

| Exception (19 CFR 134.32 unless noted) | Example of the regulatory wording |
|---|---|
| (a) Incapable of being marked | "Articles that are incapable of being marked" |
| (b) Cannot be marked before shipment without injury | "cannot be marked prior to shipment to the United States without injury" |
| (c) Marking before shipment economically prohibitive | "except at an expense economically prohibitive" |
| (d) Container marking reasonably indicates origin | "the marking of the containers will reasonably indicate the origin" |
| (e) Crude substances | "Articles which are crude substances" |
| (f) Imported for the importer's own use, not for sale | "for use by the importer and not intended for sale" |
| (i) Produced more than 20 years before importation | "produced more than 20 years prior to their importation" |
| (m) U.S. products exported and returned | "Products of the United States exported and returned" |
| 134.33 J-List (selected) | Buttons, eggs, firewood, monuments, among others |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR 134.32 and 134.33, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="origin-marking-requirements-16" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-17" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-18" />

The (f) exception is narrow for resellers: it covers goods the importer uses itself, not inventory bought to sell. Section 134.32 has further paragraphs not shown in this table; read the full section if your goods could fall within one.

## What happens to goods that are not legally marked

Subparts A and F set out the consequences.

- **Delivery is withheld.** An article held in CBP custody that is found not legally marked will not be delivered until it is marked with its country of origin, or until the estimated additional duties are deposited or adequate security for them is provided. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-21" />
- **Additional duty.** An article not legally marked at importation is subject to the 10 percent additional duty on its final appraised value, unless exported or destroyed under customs supervision before liquidation. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-20" />
- **Redelivery demand.** If goods were already released, the importer can be told to return them to customs custody for marking, exportation, or destruction. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-24" />
- **Liquidated damages.** If the importer does not mark or redeliver within 30 days from the date of the notice of redelivery, the port director demands liquidated damages equal to the entered value of the articles not properly marked or redelivered. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-22" />
- **Removing a mark is a crime.** Removing, defacing or altering a required marking may result in criminal penalties of up to $5,000 and/or imprisonment for 1 year. <C id="origin-marking-requirements-23" />

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical figures)">
Assume a shipment of unmarked kitchen tools with a final appraised value of $20,000 is not exported or destroyed before liquidation. The additional marking duty would be 10% x $20,000 = $2,000, on top of the regular duty for the tariff classification. If the goods had already been released and were not marked or returned within 30 days of a redelivery notice, the liquidated damages claim would equal their entered value.
</Callout>

## Decision table: which marking rule your selling setup triggers

The sections above follow Part 134's structure. The table below starts instead from common marketplace setups and points each one to the provision it triggers.

| If your situation is… | What applies | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| You sell the article at retail in the form it was imported | The retail buyer is the ultimate purchaser the mark must reach | 19 CFR 134.1(d)(3) <C id="origin-marking-requirements-05" /> |
| Your packaging prints a U.S. brand address or city | Country of origin marked in close proximity and at least comparable size, where the address may mislead | 19 CFR 134.46 <C id="origin-marking-requirements-11" /> |
| You rely on a sticker or hang tag | Sticker in a conspicuous place, secure enough to stay on unless deliberately removed; tag attached in a conspicuous place | 19 CFR 134.44 <C id="origin-marking-requirements-09" /> |
| Your goods are repacked in retail containers after release (for example, by a prep center) | The repacker must not obscure or conceal the mark, and the importer notifies the purchaser or transferee in writing at the time of sale | 19 CFR 134.26 <C id="origin-marking-requirements-15" /> |
| You import an insert or label that ships combined with another product | Mark shows the origin is that of the label only, such as "Label made in (country)" | 19 CFR 134.14 <C id="origin-marking-requirements-12" /> |
| A U.S. manufacturer converts your import into a different article | That manufacturer is the ultimate purchaser, and the article is excepted | 19 CFR 134.35(a) <C id="origin-marking-requirements-19" /> |
| CBP finds goods in its custody unmarked | Mark them, or deposit estimated additional duties or security, before delivery | 19 CFR 134.3(a) <C id="origin-marking-requirements-21" /> |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR Part 134, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="origin-marking-requirements-05" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-11" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-15" /> <C id="origin-marking-requirements-21" />

The second and fourth rows are the ones a private-label seller controls through artwork and prep instructions. Both can be settled before goods ship; the last row shows the choices left once CBP has found the problem.
