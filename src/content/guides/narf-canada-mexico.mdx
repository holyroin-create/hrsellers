International expansion usually means inventory, customs and compliance in a second country — as the [Europe guide](/guides/expanding-eu-uk-compliance/) sets out at length. North America has a route that skips most of that, and it is worth understanding what it costs before assuming it is free.

## The constraint it solves

Reporting states plainly that **Amazon does not support FBA fulfilment between the US, Canada and Mexico.** Without remote fulfilment, you set up FBA in each country, inventory is managed separately, and a seller cannot fulfil an order in one country with stock held in another. Reporting also notes Amazon requires different FBA SKUs for each North American selling location.

That is three inventory pools, three forecasts and three restock decisions for the same product.

Remote Fulfillment removes it: **one inventory pool in US fulfilment centres, serving orders across the marketplaces you enrol in.**

## What it requires

Reported eligibility, consistent across sources:

- A **Professional** selling account — not Individual
- A **North America unified account**. Amazon's own page now describes it as a North America **and Brazil** unified selling account
- **Enrolled in FBA in the US, with FBA Export enabled**
- **FBA only.** Merchant-fulfilled listings are not eligible, because Amazon must control the shipping to handle cross-border documentation and clearance
- **Compliance with destination-country regulations.** Restricted items, dangerous goods and prohibited products are ineligible

Most modern accounts are reported to be unified by default; older accounts may need upgrading through an Upgrade to a Unified Account page in settings.

**Enrolment is described as automatic once switched on:** Amazon evaluates your inventory, adds eligible items, and continues adding newly eligible ASINs as you create them. Build International Listings can replicate your US offers and price them automatically for exchange rates, fulfilment costs and referral fee differences.

## What it costs

**A remote fulfilment fee replaces the FBA fee**, and reporting describes it as higher than the standard US fee to reflect cross-border shipping.

**Delivery is slower.** Reported Prime delivery: **5 to 9 days for Mexico**, **7 to 12 days for Canada.** One source is direct that remote fulfilment is not ideal for Canada because of the longer delivery times and higher costs, and frames it as a way to test the market rather than a permanent arrangement.

**Customers pay import duties.** Reporting states the customer takes ownership when the shipping company collects from the US fulfilment centre. That removes the importer-of-record question from you — see the [Incoterms guide](/guides/incoterms-who-is-importer/) for why that matters — and it adds a cost the buyer sees at checkout or on delivery.

**Returns come back to your US fulfilment centre**, handled as US returns are.

## The advantage nobody mentions

One reported point is worth more attention than it gets: **advertising costs in Canada and Mexico are described as significantly lower than in the US** because of lower competition, allowing customer acquisition at a materially lower cost per click.

Against the [US CPC picture](/guides/amazon-ppc-benchmarks/) — reported at $1.00 to $1.25 blended and rising 8% to 12% year on year — that is a genuine offset to the higher fulfilment fee, and it is the strongest argument for testing these markets rather than a marginal one.

## When to graduate to local inventory

Remote fulfilment is a test mechanism. If a market works, local FBA gives you faster delivery, standard fee rates and no duty on the customer.

The trade is what you avoided: local inventory, cross-border shipments, customs clearance, and country-specific compliance. Reporting names **bilingual labelling for Canada** and **Mexican tax requirements** as examples, and notes compliance failures risk penalties or account suspension. Canada also requires consideration of non-resident importer registration and a brokerage arrangement.

**A reasonable rule:** stay remote while you are learning whether demand exists; move local when the fee difference on your actual volume exceeds the cost and complexity of holding inventory there. That is a calculation, and remote fulfilment gives you the data to make it.

## What to do

**Check your account is unified** before anything else — the language switcher in Seller Central shows whether the other marketplaces appear.

**Enable Build International Listings** with automatic pricing at enrolment. Reporting describes it as adjusting for exchange rates, fulfilment costs and referral fee differences, which is exactly the arithmetic you would otherwise do per SKU.

**Review the replicated listings anyway.** Automatic pricing preserves margin mechanically; it does not know whether the resulting price is competitive in that market.

**Check ineligible categories first.** Dangerous goods and restricted products do not qualify — see the [hazmat guide](/guides/hazmat-dangerous-goods/).

**Set expectations on delivery.** Seven to twelve days is not a US Prime experience, and it affects reviews and conversion in ways your US listing history will not predict.

**Test advertising there.** The lower reported CPCs are the part of this that is genuinely undervalued.

**Model the graduation to local inventory** before you need to. Knowing the volume at which local FBA becomes cheaper turns a vague ambition into a trigger.

**Run it on your own figures.** The [margin and break-even calculator](/tools/margin-calculator/) takes your price, cost of goods, referral rate, fulfilment fee, returns and ad spend and returns net profit per unit, net margin and the maximum ACOS the product can carry. Nothing is stored and nothing leaves your browser.
