If a product you import is covered by a Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) rule, it must travel with a certificate of compliance, and as the importer of record that certificate is yours to issue. <C id="us-product-compliance-05"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-06"/> Children's products get a Children's Product Certificate (CPC) backed by third-party lab tests; other regulated products get a General Certificate of Conformity (GCC). <C id="us-product-compliance-04"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-20"/> Under the corrected final rule, electronic filing of certificate data took effect on July 8, 2026. <C id="us-product-compliance-18"/>

## Step one: is it a "children's product"?

Everything downstream depends on this classification. Under 16 CFR 1200.2, a children's product is a consumer product designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger. <C id="us-product-compliance-01"/> CPSC does not rely on your word alone. It looks at how the product is represented in its packaging, display, promotion or advertising, <C id="us-product-compliance-02"/> and whether consumers commonly recognize it as meant for children 12 or younger. <C id="us-product-compliance-03"/>

That means listing copy, main images and the age range you enter on a marketplace are evidence. A night light sold as a general home item and the same light shaped like a cartoon animal and photographed in a nursery can land on different sides of the line.

## Who signs the certificate

For goods made abroad, the 2025 final rule puts responsibility for the finished product certificate on the importer of record (IOR). <C id="us-product-compliance-06"/> Part 1110 defines the importer as the Importer of Record eligible to make entry under the Tariff Act of 1930. <C id="us-product-compliance-07"/> If your supplier ships under DDP terms and someone else is listed as IOR, check who that is — the certificate obligation follows the IOR role, not who designed or sells the product.

You must also furnish the certificate to each distributor or retailer of the product, <C id="us-product-compliance-15"/> and it must be produced within 24 hours if CPSC or CBP asks. <C id="us-product-compliance-16"/>

## What a certificate must contain

An eFiled certificate must be in English. <C id="us-product-compliance-08"/> The data elements are fixed by 16 CFR 1110.11:

| Element | What to provide |
|---|---|
| Product identification | At least one unique identifier, such as a GTIN, model number, SKU or UPC |
| Manufacture | Date (month and year at minimum) and place of manufacture |
| Testing | Most recent date and place of testing |
| Records | Name and contact details of the person keeping the supporting records |

*Source: eCFR, 16 CFR 1110.11, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="us-product-compliance-09"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-10"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-11"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-12"/>

Part 1110 sets what the certificate says and who issues it, but it does not address the type or frequency of testing behind it. <C id="us-product-compliance-17"/> For children's products, that comes from Part 1107, covered below.

## eFiling: what changed and when

For imports, the certifier must eFile the certificate data elements when the entry is filed in ACE. <C id="us-product-compliance-13"/> If the goods arrive by mail, the data goes into CPSC's Product Registry before the product reaches the United States. <C id="us-product-compliance-14"/>

In practice this means the certificate can no longer live only in a folder you hand over after the fact. Your customs broker needs the same data elements the paper certificate carries — the product identifier, the manufacture date and place, the test date and place, and the records contact — at the moment the entry is filed. <C id="us-product-compliance-09"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-13"/> Sellers who consolidate many SKUs into one container need that data ready for every regulated product in the entry, since each one must be accompanied by a certificate. <C id="us-product-compliance-05"/> Build the data set when you book the lab test, not when the container is already on the water.

The September 2025 correction did not move these dates; it relabeled them so that July 8, 2026 is the effective date of the rule. <C id="us-product-compliance-18"/> If you store goods in a foreign trade zone before entry, January 8, 2027 is the date that matters for those entries. <C id="us-product-compliance-19"/>

## Third-party testing for children's products

Manufacturers must submit enough samples of a children's product to a third party conformity assessment body for testing to support certification. <C id="us-product-compliance-20"/> That body must be a testing laboratory whose accreditation CPSC has accepted. <C id="us-product-compliance-21"/> A report from a lab outside that accepted group does not fit this definition.

Testing is not one-and-done. All manufacturers of children's products must conduct periodic testing, <C id="us-product-compliance-22"/> and the interval depends on which option you use:

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical): a retest calendar">
Inputs: a seller imports a plush toy for toddlers. The first certification test from a CPSC-accepted lab is dated March 10, 2026, and production continues all year.

- With no production testing plan, the next periodic test must happen within one year, so by March 10, 2027 at the latest. <C id="us-product-compliance-23"/>
- With a production testing plan in place, the third-party sample can wait up to two years, so by March 10, 2028. <C id="us-product-compliance-24"/>
- If in September 2026 the factory switches to a new stuffing supplier, that is a sourcing change. If a manufacturer exercising due care knows or should know it could affect compliance, it is a material change, and the new batch needs to meet the requirements before a new CPC is issued, regardless of where the periodic calendar stands. <C id="us-product-compliance-26"/> <C id="us-product-compliance-27"/>

Each test report, and the certificate built on it, then goes into a file you keep for five years. <C id="us-product-compliance-28"/>
</Callout>

### Material changes reset the clock

A material change is any change in the product's design, manufacturing process, or sourcing of component parts that a manufacturer exercising due care knows, or should know, could affect the product's ability to comply. <C id="us-product-compliance-26"/> After one, you cannot issue a new CPC until the changed product meets the applicable requirements. <C id="us-product-compliance-27"/> Switching paint suppliers, swapping a zipper vendor, or moving production to a second factory can qualify when they could affect compliance — confirm with your lab whether a retest is needed before the new batch ships.

All of this must be documented: certification records for children's products must be kept for five years. <C id="us-product-compliance-28"/>

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Treating a factory's in-house report as the CPC basis | The lab must be one whose accreditation CPSC has accepted |
| Testing once and never again | Periodic testing is mandatory, one year by default |
| Changing a component supplier without retesting | A sourcing change that could affect compliance is a material change; no new CPC until compliance is shown |
| Leaving the identifier off | At least one unique identifier is required |
