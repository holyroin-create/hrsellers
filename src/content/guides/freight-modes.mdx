Freight guides are written by freight forwarders and they all quote rates. Those rates were correct on the day they were written and are a starting point for a conversation now, nothing more.

What does not move much is the arithmetic that tells you which mode to use.

## The rates, and how far apart they are

| Mode | Reported 2026 figures |
| --- | --- |
| **Express courier** | $6–$12 per kg · $8–$14 per kg · 3–7 days |
| **Air freight** | $4–$9/kg · $4.85–$9/kg · $5.50–$9/kg · $5–$12/kg all-in · $7.40/kg (July 2026, 1,000kg+ to LA/NY/Miami) |
| **Ocean LCL** | $110–$150/CBM · $130/CBM · $80–$220/CBM West Coast · $150–$400/CBM |
| **Ocean FCL** | $1,500–$3,500 (20ft WC) · $1,900–$3,200 (40ft WC) · $2,000–$4,500 (40ft) · $2,500–$6,500 · $5,400–$6,600 (20GP, July 2026) |

The FCL spread is more than fourfold. One source's July 2026 figures are roughly double another's March 2026 figures for comparable containers.

**Both can be right.** Reporting names Red Sea routing, Hormuz tensions and tariff-driven volatility as swinging rates and timing more than they used to. **The lesson is not which number to use — it is that a freight rate has a shelf life of weeks.**

## The thresholds, which do not move

Reported consistently, and this is the actionable part:

| Volume | Mode |
| --- | --- |
| Under ~2 CBM | Air or express — below this, port and destination charges make LCL more expensive |
| 2–15 CBM | **LCL** |
| Above ~15 CBM | **FCL** — one 40ft container is reported as often cheaper than three LCL shipments of the same total volume, and faster |

**Why LCL loses at both ends.** Below 2 CBM, the fixed origin and destination charges dominate. Above 15, you are paying consolidation and deconsolidation costs on volume that would fill a container — and waiting for both.

## Transit times

| Mode | Port to port / direct | Door to FBA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Express | — | 3–7 days |
| Air | 2–3 days direct | 8–14 days |
| Ocean FCL | 14–22 days WC, 25–35 EC | 30–45 days |
| Ocean LCL | 10–17 to 25–40 days | 35–55 days |

**Note the LCL door-to-FBA figure.** Reporting puts it at 35 to 55 days once supplier production slack, customs and prep are counted — longer than FCL, because consolidation at origin and deconsolidation at destination each add time.

**And add Amazon's own receiving:** reported at 1 to 3 business days after delivery, which is not optional in your planning even though nobody quotes it.

## The chargeable weight trap

Air freight charges on **chargeable weight** — the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight.

That formula punishes bulky, lightweight goods specifically. A carton of cushions and a carton of tools may weigh very differently and cost the same to fly, because the cushions are priced on the space they occupy.

**Which means the air-or-sea question is not only about urgency.** For dense, heavy, low-value goods, air is never viable. For light, compact, high-value goods, it is far more often viable than sellers assume.

## The split shipment

This is the technique worth taking from this guide.

Reported practice among experienced sellers: **send a small air batch — one source gives 100 to 300 kg — to start selling immediately, and the bulk by ocean to arrive three to four weeks later.**

The air batch generates sales and reviews while the ocean batch is in transit. It costs slightly more than pure ocean shipping and it prevents a launch stockout.

**Why that matters beyond the freight bill:** as the [listing guide](/guides/listing-optimisation-a10/) covers, ranking erodes before a stockout actually happens, and the [low-inventory-level fee](/guides/low-inventory-level-fee/) charges you per unit while you are thin. A launch that runs out at week three has paid for the freight saving twice over.

The same logic applies to emergency restocks. Reporting is consistent that most experienced sellers use sea for regular replenishment and air selectively.

## What a rate quote leaves out

Ocean freight is one line on a bill with eight. One reported breakdown for a 1 CBM LCL shipment from Shenzhen:

| Component | Reported |
| --- | --- |
| Ocean freight | $80–$200 |
| Origin handling | $60–$120 |
| Export customs | $30–$60 |
| Destination terminal handling | $80–$150 |
| Customs brokerage | $80–$200 |
| ISF filing | $25–$50 |
| Drayage to FBA | $80–$200 |
| Forwarder service fee | $50–$150 |
| **Total** | **$485–$1,130** |

**Import duties are additional.** So is any examination — see the [customs holds guide](/guides/customs-holds-and-exams/).

That total against an ocean freight line of $80 to $200 is the reason a cheap freight quote and a cheap shipment are different things. See the [forwarder guide](/guides/choosing-a-freight-forwarder/).

## What to do

**Calculate your CBM first.** Volume decides the mode before price does. Length × width × height in metres, per carton, times carton count.

**Get quotes dated this week.** Anything older is a range, not a price.

**Ask for the all-in number**, not the freight line. The 1 CBM breakdown above is why.

**Check chargeable weight before dismissing air.** For compact, high-value goods the gap is smaller than the headline per-kg figures suggest.

**Plan the split on launches and on anything with a stockout risk.** Air batch to sell, ocean batch to sustain.

**Add Amazon receiving to every timeline.** One to three business days, after delivery, before anything sells.

**Build freight into landed cost per unit**, not as a shipment-level expense — see the [landed cost guide](/guides/landed-cost-fee-stack/).

**Model it on your own figures.** The [cash flow planner](/tools/cash-flow-planner/) maps deposit, balance, freight, receiving and payout onto a timeline so you can see the gap between money leaving and money returning. Nothing is stored and nothing leaves your browser.
