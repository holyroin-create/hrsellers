One percent. Cross it and Amazon can deactivate the account on that metric alone, and the notification arrives after you have crossed, not while you are drifting toward it. Every metric Amazon publishes has a threshold. This one is different in two ways: it as capable of suspending an account **on its own**, regardless of how everything else looks, and the warning system is telling you after the fact.

## What counts as a defect

Three signals, on a rolling 60-day window:

- **Negative feedback** — only 1-star and 2-star. A 3-star rating is neutral and does not count
- **A-to-z Guarantee claims** filed against you — Amazon's ODR page defines which outcomes count, and agency guides disagree on whether a denied claim drops out; check your own Account Health page rather than assuming
- **Service chargebacks**

**Multiple negative signals on the same order count as one defect, not two or three.** A buyer who leaves 1-star feedback and files an A-to-z claim on the same order produces one defect.

Note also that since August 2025, buyers can submit **star-only ratings with no written comment** — which means a defect can appear with no explanation attached and nothing to diagnose from.

## The small-seller problem

This is the structural point nobody states plainly.

ODR is a percentage, and on a rolling 60-day window. **At low order volume, each defect carries far more statistical weight.**

At 1,000 orders in 60 days, one defect is 0.1%. At 100 orders, one defect is 1% — the threshold, from a single unhappy customer. this directly: a string of negative reviews from a bad batch can push ODR past 1% inside a week, especially for sellers with low order volume.

**Which means new and small sellers are structurally more exposed to this metric than large ones**, and the standard advice to "keep ODR low" is not equally achievable at every scale. The practical response is not better averages — it is preventing individual defects, because at low volume each one matters.

## The warning problem

the notification system as reactive: **you get an alert after your ODR exceeds 1%, not when it is trending toward it at 0.7%.**

The metric moves on a 60-day window, so by the time the alert arrives, the defects causing it are up to two months old and there is nothing to undo. The window is short enough that a bad week of holiday orders can spike your rate before you have time to react.

**The answer is your own monitoring, at a cadence Amazon does not provide.** One published routine takes under fifteen minutes a week:

1. Account Health dashboard **daily** — two minutes
2. Feedback Manager **weekly** for new negative entries
3. A-to-z claims **immediately** — do not let them auto-grant
4. Track which SKUs generate defects

That fourth item is the one that changes outcomes. tracking defect-generating SKUs as the fastest way to lower ODR, because defects cluster by product rather than distributing evenly.

## Preventing the claim rather than winning it

If a denied claim stays in your rate — and you should verify this on your Account Health page rather than assume it — then defending a claim successfully still leaves the defect.

A-to-z claims as following a predictable pattern — **shipment delayed, seller unresponsive, buyer escalates** — that can be interrupted before it reaches the claim stage.

Two things follow.

**Respond to buyer messages within 24 hours.** this as directly affecting ODR and being tracked, rather than being a courtesy standard.

**Refund proactively before a claim is filed.** a proactive refund prevents the claim existing in your ODR at all. On a $25 item, refunding to avoid a defect is cheap insurance — particularly at low order volume where one defect is a large percentage.

That is an uncomfortable conclusion for anyone who believes in defending the position, and it is the arithmetic.

## Feedback removal

Amazon automatically removing feedback that:

- Contains a product review rather than seller experience feedback
- Contains abusive language
- For FBA orders, complains about shipping that was Amazon's responsibility

Buyers also have 60 days to remove feedback themselves.

**That third category is worth knowing.** An FBA seller receiving negative feedback about late delivery has grounds, because Amazon shipped it. Reviewing negative feedback for removable entries is a legitimate and reasonably quick task.

## Account Health Rating

the AHR as consolidating compliance into one score:

| Score | Status |
| --- | --- |
| 200+ | Healthy — where a new account starts |
| 100–199 | At risk |
| Below 100 | Can trigger suspension |

It is calculated across customer service performance, policy compliance and shipping performance for merchant-fulfilled orders.

Two operational notes from reporting. **Pulling an ASIN without addressing the underlying violation is the most common mistake** — it leaves the point deduction in place. And **delayed responses to violations can result in additional deductions**, so a Plan of Action submitted within 24 hours is better than a better one submitted next week.

## The other metrics, briefly

| Metric | Threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Order Defect Rate | Below 1% |
| Late Shipment Rate | Under 4% — two consecutive months above typically triggers a notification |
| Valid Tracking Rate | Above 95% |
| Cancellation Rate | Amazon-set |
| On-Time Delivery Rate | Tightened for Prime-eligible sellers in early 2026, per agency reports |
| Feedback rating | Aim 95%+; below 90% affects Buy Box win rate |

**For FBA-only sellers**, Amazon handles the shipping metrics, so ODR and policy compliance dominate. That is one genuine argument for FBA that does not appear in fee comparisons — see the [FBA against FBM guide](/guides/fba-vs-fbm-breakeven/).

Reporting also links feedback to the Buy Box: during high-traffic periods, sellers rated 4.5 stars or above are winning the Buy Box roughly 79% of the time, with a drop below 4.3 affecting it. See the [Buy Box guide](/guides/buy-box-featured-offer/).

## What to do

**Check the Account Health dashboard daily.** Two minutes, and it is the only early warning that exists.

**Set your own alert level at 0.5%**, not 1%. The published threshold is the cliff edge, not the target.

**Respond to buyer messages within 24 hours**, without exception.

**Refund before a claim is filed** where the amount is small relative to the cost of a defect.

**Track defects by SKU.** They cluster, and one product usually explains most of them.

**Review negative feedback for removable entries** — product reviews, abusive content, and FBA shipping complaints.

**If you are above 0.8%, stop everything else.** The right order is to audit the last 60 days of claims, feedback and chargebacks before working on anything else. It is the one metric that suspends an account by itself.
