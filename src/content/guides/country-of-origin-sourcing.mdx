U.S. customs law does not take the "ship from" country on your invoice as the origin. For most goods, the country of origin is where the article was manufactured, produced or grown, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-01" /> and processing in a second country changes that answer only if it is a "substantial transformation." <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" /> For goods of a NAFTA or USMCA country, the regulation instead sends you to a separate rule set in 19 CFR Part 102, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-03" /> and textiles and apparel have their own Part 102 rules. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-08" />

The answer matters because many duties are tied to origin rather than to the product alone. The Section 301 duties, for example, were imposed on products of China, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-12" /> and antidumping orders are listed by product and country. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-16" /> Moving final assembly to another country is a sourcing decision that only changes the duty if it also changes origin under the applicable rule.

<Callout type="note" title="Not a ruling">
This page explains how origin is worked out. It cannot tell you the origin of your product; that turns on your actual production steps, which a CBP ruling or a licensed customs broker can assess.
</Callout>

## The baseline: substantial transformation (19 CFR 134.1)

The definitions in 19 CFR 134.1 set the general test used for country of origin marking. "Country of origin" means "the country of manufacture, production, or growth of any article of foreign origin." <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-01" /> If further work or material is added in another country, it "must effect a substantial transformation" for that other country to become the origin. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" />

The regulation does not turn substantial transformation into a formula. A related marking provision, 19 CFR 134.35(a), describes the kind of change involved: a new article with "a name, character, or use differing" from the imported article. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-15" /> That wording is the closest thing in Part 134 to a working description of the change involved.

## When Part 102 applies instead

The same 134.1(b) definition carves out goods of a NAFTA or USMCA country: for those, origin for marking is decided by the rules in Part 102. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-03" /> Note that NAFTA does not apply to goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, on or after July 1, 2020, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-04" /> so for current entries the USMCA references are the operative ones.

Part 102's own scope statement says that, except for 102.21 through 102.25, it sets rules for determining origin for the purposes listed in Annex 311 of NAFTA, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-05" /> which are country of origin marking and determining the rate of duty and staging category for originating textile and apparel products and for originating goods under Annex 302.2. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-06" /> Sections 102.1 through 102.18 and 102.20 also determine origin for marking purposes under the USMCA. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-07" />

The core test is in 19 CFR 102.11(a). Origin is the country in which the good is wholly obtained or produced, the good is produced exclusively from domestic materials, or each foreign material in the good undergoes an applicable change in tariff classification. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-09" /> Part 102 sets out "Specific rules by tariff classification" in 102.20, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-17" /> which is why origin under Part 102 depends on classification; see [HTS classification basics](/guides/hts-classification-basics/).

## Textile and apparel products (19 CFR 102.21)

Textiles follow a separate section. 19 CFR 102.21 governs the country of origin of imported textile and apparel products for "the Customs laws and the administration of quantitative restrictions," for goods entered or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption on or after July 1, 1996. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-08" />

## Which rule applies where

| Your goods | Rule set | Where it is written |
|---|---|---|
| Most non-textile goods, for marking | Country of manufacture, production or growth; substantial transformation for work in a second country | 19 CFR 134.1(b) |
| Goods of a NAFTA or USMCA country, for marking | Part 102 rules (wholly obtained, domestic materials, or tariff shift) | 19 CFR 134.1(b); 102.0; 102.11 |
| Textile and apparel products entered on or after July 1, 1996 | Five-step hierarchy for the customs laws | 19 CFR 102.21 |

## Why origin drives the duty rate

Origin is the switch that turns several duty layers on or off. Two examples you can read in the Federal Register:

**Section 301 duties on products of China.** USTR's [May 6, 2026 notice opening the second four-year review](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/06/2026-08806/initiation-of-second-four-year-review-process-chinas-acts-policies-and-practices-related-to) recounts that the first action imposed additional duties of 25 percent ad valorem on 818 tariff subheadings with an approximate annual trade value of $34 billion, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-11" /> and the second imposed additional duties of 25 percent on 279 tariff subheadings with about $16 billion in trade, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-13" /> effective July 6, 2018, and August 23, 2018. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-12" /> In its [October 7, 2026 continuation notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/07/2026-20510/continuation-of-actions-chinas-acts-policies-and-practices-related-to-technology-transfer), USTR stated that the July 6, 2018 and August 23, 2018 actions, as modified, did not terminate and remain in effect. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-14" />

The May 2026 notice also records additional duties on supplemental lists of products, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-12" /> so the current rate for a given subheading has to be checked against the current notices and the tariff schedule. The layering of these duties is covered in [The 2026 tariff stack](/guides/tariff-stack-2026/).

**Antidumping duties by country.** Commerce's [September 1, 2026 notice of opportunity to request administrative review](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/01/2026-17810/antidumping-or-countervailing-duty-order-finding-or-suspended-investigation-opportunity-to-request) lists the mattress orders separately for India (A-533-919), Mexico (A-201-859), Spain (A-469-826) and Kosovo (A-803-001). <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-16" /> The same product carries a different case for each origin country. See [Antidumping and countervailing duties](/guides/antidumping-duties/).

## Sourcing decisions this affects

- **Final assembly in a second country.** Only a substantial transformation (or, for USMCA goods, the Part 102 rules) moves origin; <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" /> work in a second country that falls short of that leaves origin where it was.
- **Mixed-material products.** Under Part 102, each foreign material is tested for the required classification change, <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-09" /> so a bill of materials with HTS numbers is the starting document.
- **Marking follows the same answer.** The country you determine here is the one that has to appear on the product under [19 CFR Part 134](/guides/origin-marking-requirements/).
- **Valuation is separate.** Origin decides which duties apply; the value they are charged on is a separate question covered in [First-sale valuation](/guides/first-sale-valuation/).

## Worked example (hypothetical): what moving assembly does to the Section 301 layer

This example shows how to frame a relocation decision using only the origin rules and the Section 301 rate on this page. All inputs are hypothetical.

**Inputs.** A seller imports a product of China classified in a subheading covered by the July 6, 2018 action, and the example assumes that action's 25 percent rate applies to it. Products outside the covered subheadings are not subject to this layer. One shipment has a value of $40,000. The supplier offers to move final assembly to a second country for an extra $4,000 per shipment.

| Scenario | Country of origin | Section 301 additional duty on this shipment |
|---|---|---|
| A. Keep assembly in China | China | 25% x $40,000 = $10,000 <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-11" /> |
| B. Move assembly; the work is a substantial transformation | Second country | Not applicable, since the actions cover products of China <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" /> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-12" /> |
| C. Move assembly; the work falls short of substantial transformation | China | Still $10,000, plus the $4,000 relocation cost <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" /> |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register, USTR notice of May 6, 2026, and eCFR, 19 CFR 134.1, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-11" /> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02" />

**Reading the result.** Scenario B saves $10,000 - $4,000 = $6,000 per shipment, but only if the second-country work changes origin. Scenario C is the worst case: the seller pays for relocation and keeps the duty. The decision therefore turns on the transformation question, not on the supplier's address.

Two further checks belong in the same calculation. First, the 25% figure is the rate set by the first two actions, which remain in effect as modified; supplemental lists also exist, so confirm whether your subheading is covered and its current rate. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-11" /> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-13" /> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-14" /> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-12" /> Second, a new origin can bring its own layers: the mattress orders, for example, are listed separately for India, Mexico, Spain and Kosovo. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-16" />
