U.S. customs treats a product as coming from the country where it was manufactured, produced or grown, and a second country becomes the origin only if the work done there effects a substantial transformation. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-01"/> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-02"/> That single determination decides whether China-specific duties apply: USTR's 2018 Section 301 actions remain in effect, and since July 24, 2026 products of China also carry a separate 12.5 percent forced-labor tariff. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-17"/> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-23"/> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-24"/> Moving a supplier, or a final step of production, only changes your duty if it changes origin under these rules.

## Why origin drives your duty bill

The extra layers discussed here are country-specific. The forced-labor action, for example, is written as tariffs "on products of China." <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-23"/> U.S. note 52, which implements those duties, says that, except as provided in its subdivisions (b) through (k), the products "shall also be subject to" the general rates of duty and to any additional duty provided for in that subchapter or in subchapter IV of chapter 99. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-25"/> So an origin answer of "China" can bring more than one layer, while "Vietnam" or "Mexico" removes the China-specific ones and may bring others.

For goods of a USMCA country, Part 134 itself points to the Part 102 marking rules. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-03"/> Sections 102.1 through 102.18 and 102.20 determine origin for marking purposes under the USMCA. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-04"/> The 102.21 rules for textile and apparel products apply for the purposes listed in Part 102 and for other purposes stated in that section. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-05"/>

## Substantial transformation in practice

The regulation states the test in terms of results. When an article is used in U.S. manufacturing that produces an article with a different name, character or use, the U.S. manufacturer becomes the ultimate purchaser of the import. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-11"/> For processing abroad, a useful way to frame the facts is the same: did the work in the second country produce a new and different article, or did it leave the original article's identity intact?

Part 134 offers no bright-line list. That is the point to plan around: a supplier's "assembled in Vietnam" label does not settle the question. What matters is what the components were when they arrived in the second country, and what came out.

<Callout type="tip" title="Questions to ask your supplier">
Which components come from which country? What happens to them in the final country — cutting, molding, circuit programming, or only screwing parts together? Does the finished product have a different name and use from the parts? Get the answers in writing with a bill of materials before you rely on a new origin.
</Callout>

## When the Part 102 rules apply

Part 102 runs a hierarchy. Origin is the country where the good is wholly obtained or produced; if not, where it is produced exclusively from domestic materials; if not, where each foreign material undergoes an applicable change in tariff classification. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-06"/> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-07"/> <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-08"/> Section 102.11 covers goods other than textile and apparel products under 102.21. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-09"/>

Part 102 also defines "simple assembly" as fitting together five or fewer parts, all of which are foreign (fasteners such as screws and bolts excluded), by bolting, gluing, soldering, sewing or other means without more than minor processing. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-10"/> If your product is a handful of imported parts snapped together, expect that definition to come up.

Inputs: a $20,000 shipment of desk organizers, a 4% base rate, and these assumptions: the item is on the July 2018 list (25%), is subject to the forced-labor action (12.5%), falls under none of note 52's subdivisions (b) through (k), has no exclusion, and the three rates simply add up. ## Building an origin file you can defend

The declared origin on your entry has to be supportable when CBP asks. For a product that touches more than one country, keep a short origin file for each SKU:

- A bill of materials listing each component, its supplier and its country.
- A description of every production step and where it takes place, with photos or a process flow from the factory.
- The HTS classification of the finished good and of the main components, which you need anyway if the Part 102 tariff-shift rules apply. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-08"/>
- The basis for your conclusion: wholly produced, produced from domestic materials, tariff shift, or substantial transformation.
- Any CBP ruling you rely on, with a note on how your product compares to the one in the ruling.

Update the file when the supplier changes a component source. A switch of one key part from a local supplier to a Chinese one can change the analysis even if nothing else in the factory changes. The same file supports the origin printed on the product under the marking rules, so the two never drift apart. <C id="country-of-origin-sourcing-01"/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Relabeling is not transformation">
Shipping finished Chinese goods through another country and changing the label does not change origin. It can also bring antidumping circumvention findings; see the [antidumping duties guide](/guides/antidumping-duties/).
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this guide">
Origin calls on mixed-country products are fact-specific. The way to get a binding answer for a planned sourcing change is a CBP ruling request describing your actual production steps.
</Callout>

## How to confirm it for your goods

Read 19 CFR 134.1 and, if your goods are from a USMCA country or are textiles, 19 CFR Part 102 on the eCFR. Then check the Section 301 notices on federalregister.gov under the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative, including the October 7, 2026 continuation notice, the December 1, 2025 exclusion extension (and any later one) and the July 28, 2026 forced-labor notice, for your exact HTS subheading. Pair that with the [origin marking guide](/guides/origin-marking-requirements/), because the origin you declare is also the one you must print on the goods.
