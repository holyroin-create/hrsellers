US customs law gives importers two separate correction tools, and which one you use depends on who is wrong. If you made the error, for example an undervalued invoice or a wrong classification that underpaid duty, the tool is a **prior disclosure** under 19 CFR 162.74. It means disclosing the circumstances of the violation before, or without knowledge of, a formal investigation and tendering the lost duties. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-01" /> If CBP made a decision you disagree with, such as a reclassification or a refused drawback claim, the tool is a **protest** under 19 CFR Part 174. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-15" /> For entries made on or after December 18, 2004, it is due within 180 days of the decision. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-17" />

Both run against the same backdrop: **liquidation**, CBP's final computation of the duties on an entry. This page covers the timing of each.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of this page">
This page summarizes how prior disclosures and protests work. Deadlines run from dates on your own entries, so check them on your entry records or with your broker.
</Callout>

## Liquidation: when an entry becomes final

The regulation defines liquidation as the final computation or ascertainment of duties on entries for consumption or drawback entries. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-11" /> For formal entries, notice of liquidation is provided on CBP's public website, and that posting is deemed the legal evidence of liquidation. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-12" /> The posting date matters because the protest clock runs from the date of notice of liquidation. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-17" />

If CBP does nothing, the entry still becomes final. An entry not liquidated within one year from the date of entry is deemed liquidated at the rate, value, quantity and amount of duties asserted by the importer of record. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-13" /> CBP can extend the one-year period by up to one year at a time, with total extensions capped at three years. An entry not liquidated within four years is deemed liquidated unless liquidation is suspended by statute or court order. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-14" />

| Liquidation event | Rule in 19 CFR Part 159 |
|---|---|
| Normal liquidation | Notice posted on CBP's public website; posting is legal evidence of liquidation |
| No action within 1 year of entry | Deemed liquidated at the importer's asserted rate, value, quantity and duty |
| Extension | Up to 1 additional year at a time; total extensions not more than 3 years |
| Outer limit | Deemed liquidated after 4 years unless liquidation is suspended |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR Part 159, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-12" /> <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-13" /> <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-14" />

## Prior disclosure: correcting your own error

### What the disclosure must say

The disclosure must identify the merchandise and the entries or drawback claims involved. It must specify the material false statements, omissions or acts, explaining how and when they occurred. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-03" /> Facts you do not yet have can follow: the disclosing party may provide information unknown at the time of disclosure within 30 days, and extensions of that 30-day period may be requested from the Fines, Penalties, and Forfeitures Officer. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-04" />

A written disclosure should be addressed to the Commissioner of Customs and presented to a Customs officer at the port of entry where the violation occurred. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-07" /> An oral disclosure counts only if it is confirmed in writing within 10 days. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-02" /> A disclosure sent by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, is deemed made at the time of mailing; one sent any other way, including in-person delivery, is deemed made when Customs receives it. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-06" />

### Paying the lost duty

The duties owed can be tendered with the disclosure or within 30 days after CBP notifies you in writing of its calculation. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-05" />

### The timing trap: formal investigations

Prior disclosure only works if you were unaware of a formal investigation. A formal investigation is considered commenced on the date Customs recorded in writing that facts caused it to believe a violation might exist. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-08" /> That date can come before you hear anything. The disclosing party carries the burden of proving it did not know of the investigation. Knowledge is presumed if the merchandise was seized or Customs issued a prepenalty or penalty notice. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-09" />

### What it is worth

With a valid prior disclosure, a fraud penalty is one times the loss of duties, taxes and fees, or 10 percent of dutiable value if there was no loss. For negligence or gross negligence, the penalty is limited to interest. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-10" />

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical inputs)">
An importer finds that negligent undervaluation on several entries caused $6,000 in lost duty. With a valid prior disclosure, the importer tenders the $6,000 and the negligence penalty is limited to interest on that amount. If the error had been fraud with prior disclosure, the penalty would be one times the loss: $6,000, on top of the tender.
</Callout>

## Protests: challenging a CBP decision

### What you can protest

Protestable decisions include the appraised value of merchandise; the classification and rate and amount of duties; the liquidation or reliquidation of an entry; and the refusal to pay a drawback claim. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-15" /> For classification disputes, background is in [HTS classification basics](/guides/hts-classification-basics/). Drawback refusals link back to [duty drawback](/guides/duty-drawback/).

### Who can file

Protests may be filed by the importer or consignee shown on the entry papers or their sureties, by any person filing a drawback claim, and by authorized agents, among others. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-16" /> Paper protests use CBP Form 19, and electronic filing through CBP-authorized systems is accepted. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-23" />

### Deadline and contents

For entries made on or after December 18, 2004, the protest is due within 180 days of the decision. The clock starts on the date of notice of liquidation or reliquidation, the date of liquidation itself, or the date of a decision that involves neither. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-17" /> The protest must state the protestant's name and address, importer number, entry number and date, liquidation date, a specific description of the merchandise, and the grounds for each objection. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-18" />

### What happens next

CBP generally must act on a protest within 2 years from filing. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-19" /> If you need an answer sooner, you can request accelerated disposition. If CBP does not act within 30 days after that request is mailed, the protest is deemed denied at the close of the 30th day. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-20" /> You may request further review where the decision conflicts with a prior ruling on similar merchandise, raises unresolved questions of law or fact, or rests on new facts or arguments. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-21" /> After a denial, the protestant may file a civil action in the US Court of International Trade within 180 days. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-22" />

| Step | Time limit | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| File protest | 180 days from the decision (entries on or after Dec 18, 2004) | 19 CFR 174.12 |
| CBP decides | Generally within 2 years of filing | 19 CFR 174.21 |
| Accelerated disposition | Deemed denied if no action 30 days after the request is mailed | 19 CFR 174.22 |
| Court of International Trade | Within 180 days after denial | 19 CFR 174.31 |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR Part 174, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-17" /> <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-19" /> <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-20" /> <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-22" />

## Which tool fits which problem

- **You underdeclared value or duty:** prior disclosure, before any formal investigation and before a seizure or penalty notice. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-09" />
- **CBP reclassified your goods or raised the value at liquidation:** protest within 180 days. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-17" />
- **CBP refused a drawback claim:** protest. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-15" />
- **Nobody acted for a year:** the entry is deemed liquidated as you declared it. <C id="prior-disclosure-protests-13" />

## What to check yourself

1. Read 19 CFR 162.74 on eCFR, especially paragraphs (a), (b), (c) and (i), before preparing a disclosure.
2. Read 19 CFR 162.73(b) to confirm the penalty levels that apply with a prior disclosure.
3. Check the liquidation posting date for each entry and calculate 180 days from it, per 19 CFR 174.12(e).
4. Confirm the decision you want to challenge is listed in 19 CFR 174.11.
5. Confirm your protest contains every element listed in 19 CFR 174.13.
6. Read 19 CFR 159.11 and 159.12 to see whether any entry has been extended or has passed the one-year deemed liquidation date.
