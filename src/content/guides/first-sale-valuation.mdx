Customs duty on most imports is charged on transaction value: the price actually paid or payable for the merchandise when sold for exportation to the United States, plus specific additions. <C id="first-sale-valuation-02" /> "First sale" is the idea that, when goods pass through a middleman before reaching you, the value can be based on the earlier sale from the factory to the middleman instead of the middleman's price to you. CBP described its interpretation in 2008 as basing transaction value on "the first or earlier sale," <C id="first-sale-valuation-12" /> provided that sale is at arm's length and the goods are clearly destined for the United States. <C id="first-sale-valuation-13" />

Because ad valorem duties, including the Section 301 and other additional duties discussed in [The 2026 tariff stack](/guides/tariff-stack-2026/), are percentages of value, a lower appraised value lowers every layer at once. The rest of this page sets out what the regulations and Federal Register documents actually say.

<Callout type="note" title="Not a ruling">
This page explains the valuation rules and the history of the first-sale position. Whether a particular supply chain qualifies depends on its documents, which a customs broker or trade attorney can review.
</Callout>

## The order of valuation methods

19 CFR 152.101(b) says imported merchandise will be appraised on the basis, and in the order, of: transaction value (152.103), transaction value of identical merchandise, transaction value of similar merchandise (both 152.104), deductive value (152.105), computed value (152.106), and the value provided for in 152.107. <C id="first-sale-valuation-01" /> Transaction value comes first; the other methods are used only when it cannot be.

## Transaction value: the price plus additions

Under 152.103(b)(1), transaction value is the price actually paid or payable for the merchandise when sold for exportation to the United States, plus the listed additions. <C id="first-sale-valuation-02" /> The definition in 152.102(f) makes the price "the total payment (whether direct or indirect," <C id="first-sale-valuation-03" /> and 152.103(a)(1) says the price is considered "without regard to its method of derivation." <C id="first-sale-valuation-04" />

| Added to the price (152.103(b)(1)) | Not part of transaction value if identified separately from the price (examples in 152.103) |
|---|---|
| (i) Packing costs incurred by the buyer | Transportation of the merchandise after importation (152.103(i)) |
| (ii) Selling commissions incurred by the buyer | Customs duties and other Federal taxes currently payable (152.103(i)(2)) |
| (iii) The apportioned value of any assist | C.I.F. charges, per Example 4 in 152.103(a)(1) |
| (iv) Royalties or license fees related to the merchandise that the buyer is required to pay | |
| (v) Proceeds of any subsequent resale, disposal or use that accrue | |

*Source: Office of the Federal Register / National Archives (eCFR), 19 CFR 152.103, checked Nov 8, 2026.* <C id="first-sale-valuation-05" /> <C id="first-sale-valuation-06" /> <C id="first-sale-valuation-07" />

Two of those additions trip up private-label sellers:

- **Assists.** An "assist" is something supplied directly or indirectly, free of charge or at reduced cost, <C id="first-sale-valuation-10" /> and the definition covers "tools, dies, molds, and similar items" used in production. <C id="first-sale-valuation-21" /> Molds or tooling you pay for and send to the factory fall here. Their value is added to the price even if it never appears on the invoice.
- **Packing.** "Packing costs" means the cost of all containers (exclusive of instruments of international traffic) and related packing, <C id="first-sale-valuation-11" /> so retail boxes you pay for separately belong in the value.

### When transaction value cannot be used

Transaction value is the appraised value only if, among other conditions, there are no restrictions on the buyer's disposition or use of the merchandise, and the buyer and seller are not related, or are related but the transaction value is acceptable. <C id="first-sale-valuation-08" /> The regulation also says the Center director shall not disregard a transaction value solely because the buyer and seller are related. <C id="first-sale-valuation-09" /> Related persons include, for example, members of the same family, such as brothers and sisters. <C id="first-sale-valuation-14" />

## What "first sale" means in the Federal Register record

The first-sale concept is explained in CBP's Federal Register documents on the phrase "sold for exportation to the United States."

- **Series of sales.** CBP's January 24, 2008 notice defines it plainly: "A series of sales consists of two or more successive contracts for sales of goods." <C id="first-sale-valuation-15" />
- **CBP's interpretation at that time.** The same notice states CBP's then-current interpretation was to base transaction value on the price paid by the buyer in the first or earlier sale. <C id="first-sale-valuation-12" />
- **Conditions.** The importer had to establish by sufficient evidence that the first sale was an arm's length sale and that the merchandise was clearly destined for exportation to the United States. <C id="first-sale-valuation-13" />
- **Case law.** The notice describes *Nissho Iwai* (Fed. Cir. 1992) as basing value on the sale between the intermediary and the manufacturer "so long as that sale constitutes a viable transaction value." <C id="first-sale-valuation-16" />

The 2008 notice proposed to use instead "the price paid in the last sale" before the goods were introduced into the United States. <C id="first-sale-valuation-22" /> CBP withdrew the proposal on September 29, 2010, citing the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008, which stated that CBP should not implement any change to its existing interpretation before January 1, 2011. <C id="first-sale-valuation-17" />

## The first-sale declaration requirement

Congress also required CBP to collect information on first-sale use for a limited time. The December 30, 2010 technical correction records that the collection ran for a one-year period beginning August 20, 2008 and ending August 19, 2009, <C id="first-sale-valuation-18" /> implemented by an interim rule published on August 25, 2008 as CBP Dec. 08-31. <C id="first-sale-valuation-19" /> Because the statutory authority expired on August 19, 2009, CBP removed paragraph (g) from 19 CFR 141.61, effective December 30, 2010. <C id="first-sale-valuation-20" />

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example (hypothetical figures)">
A factory sells 1,000 units to a trading company at $6.00 each ($6,000). The trading company resells to a U.S. importer at $8.00 each ($8,000). The importer paid $500 for a mold sent to the factory, used only for this order. Assume a combined ad valorem duty of 30%.

- Middleman-price basis: ($8,000 + $500 assist) x 30% = $2,550.
- First-sale basis, if the factory sale qualifies: ($6,000 + $500 assist) x 30% = $1,950.

The difference is $600. The assist is added in both cases because it is an addition under 152.103(b)(1)(iii). Whether the factory sale qualifies depends on the arm's-length and destined-for-the-U.S. conditions, and on documents showing that sale.
</Callout>

## Records a first-sale claim depends on

The conditions described in the 2008 notice put the burden on the importer to show the earlier sale with "sufficient evidence." <C id="first-sale-valuation-13" /> In practice that means having access to documents from a transaction you were not a party to: the factory's invoice to the middleman, proof of payment, and evidence that the goods were made for and shipped to the United States. A middleman unwilling to share its purchase price makes the approach unavailable.
